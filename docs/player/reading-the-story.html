Reading the story

Your village keeps running whether you're watching or not. The chronicle is how you catch up on everything that happened while you were away.

The chronicle: your village's own storyteller

Because the world never pauses on its own, promptworld needs a way to hand you a readable summary when you come back after a day, a week, or longer. That's the chronicle: a cloud AI model periodically reads through everything that happened and writes it up as a few short story entries — a chapter or two per game day, not a blow-by-blow transcript. It covers things like: deaths, buildings going up, sightings and attacks by the "gru" (a nocturnal threat that stalks the village at night), conversations (with a gist of what was discussed), rumors spreading, gifts given, promises broken, thefts from a chest, passing musings, and the village's own self-governance — meetings, grievances aired, proposals voted up or down, and any exiles. It also covers a villager's own sense of the map: going looking for something remembered and finding it gone, swapping directions with another villager in conversation, and a place a guardian vision revealed to someone. And if it ever comes to that, the chronicle records the end of the run itself: when the last villager dies, the story closes with a plain statement that the village stands empty. On a world created to run a specific practice exercise (see the stage pages), passing that exercise earns its own chapter too, naming what was proven. Each entry is stamped with the in-world time it covers, so the story reads in order.

A day or night that has nothing notable in it costs nothing and gets no filler entry — you only get chapters when something actually happened.

The chronicle travels with your world's save data, so the moment you reattach — no matter how long you were gone — the recent story is already there waiting, with no separate step needed to fetch it. If the narrator ever has a hiccup, the unwritten lines simply carry over to the next chapter instead of getting lost, and if the village has no AI configured at all, you just don't get narrated chapters — the raw event feed described below still works.

Where to read it

  • The full-screen client's Chronicle pane — the default tab, right there when you open promptworld ui demo. It shows the narrated story with filters by villager and by storyline thread. Press r to flip to the raw event-by-event feed instead (this is also the automatic fallback if no narrated entries exist yet); press it again to flip back. See the screen-orientation page for what the rest of that screen is showing you, and the keys reference card for the full key list.
  • The command line — promptworld tail demo --follow streams the raw event feed live in your terminal (drop --follow to just see recent history, which works even with the world not currently running). promptworld attach demo gives you the same kind of live raw feed alongside simple typed commands.
  • A plain text file — chronicle.md, sitting right in the world's save directory, regenerated automatically. You can open it in any text editor, no game required.

Reading the raw feed

When you're looking at the raw feed instead of the narrated chronicle, each line reads as a short, plain-language summary of one thing that happened — for example "Ash foraged at (14,9)" or a line of dialogue shown as "Ash→Rowan: ...". A handful of especially important moments (a death, an attack, a chest theft, a village-law violation) are visually called out so they don't slip by in a busy feed. You'll also see a villager's own knowledge of the map show up as its own lines: spotting something new ("Ash saw fire at (14,9)"), sharing directions in conversation ("Ash told Birch of fire at (14,9)"), returning to find a remembered spot has changed ("Ash found fire at (14,9) gone"), and a guardian vision that revealed a place outright ("Guardian revealed fire at (14,9) to Ash"). When a villager's building work doesn't pan out — say, the site it needed stops being usable partway through — the feed shows a distinct failure line naming the villager, what they were building, and why it failed (for example, "Ash's build_wall_stone failed — site no longer buildable"), so it reads clearly as a setback rather than looking like the build just quietly finished. If your villagers are AI-driven, you'll also occasionally see a governor shed or governor recovered line — that's the world automatically dialing its own speed down a notch to keep villagers' thinking sensible, then dialing back up once it catches up; see the time & speed page for the full story. If you've asked the guardian to watch for something and act later, you'll also see lines like "Guardian set a watch…" marking when a standing order is placed, fires, is cancelled, or lapses unused. In the full-screen client's guardian pane — or its full-screen guardian console (press G), the document-style reading view for the same conversation — the transcript itself marks a landed vision or omen, a placed or released standing order, and a pause/resume/speed change the guardian made on your behalf with their own small icons alongside one another. In the full-screen client, pausing the world lets you select any entry and see its full raw detail underneath. From there, the detail view now offers one more thing: press ⏎ on the selected entry, or click its line with the mouse (the game's first control you can also use with a mouse instead of the keyboard), and the map pans to wherever that event happened — the same recentering pan that c already uses. Not every event happened somewhere on the map (a conversation, for instance) — for those, you'll see an honest "no location for this event" instead of a jump. If you've turned on the still-being-tested memory feature described on the AI behind the village page, you'll also see a few extra bookkeeping lines go by — a memory or a villager's situation getting its "meaning fingerprint" computed, and a note about how a smarter memory pick would have compared to today's pick. These are there for the team to review evidence before turning that feature on for real; there's nothing in them you need to read or act on.

The morgue: every death leaves an epitaph

Alongside the chronicle, your world keeps one more readable document: morgue.md, sitting in the world's save folder right next to chronicle.md. It exists from the world's first day (opening with a one-line "no one has died" note while everyone lives), and every villager who dies gets a factual epitaph in it: which day they died and what killed them, the deeds they'll be remembered for — things built, gifts given, promises broken, brushes with the gru — what they carried in memory, who mattered to them, and any debts still open, in both directions, when they fell.

Each epitaph also records what your instructions were at that moment: which version of your charter was in force when the villager died — whether that was still the starting default or your own writing — and which standing orders you had active, watching for what. It's stated plainly as evidence, instruction and outcome side by side; the morgue never scores you, grades you, or assigns blame. Every epitaph closes the same way: stated as evidence — the reader draws the lesson.

If the run ends — the last villager dies — the morgue closes with a village-level summary of the whole run: how long it lasted, how the population declined day by day, every death with its cause, and the run's most notable events. On a world running a practice exercise, that summary also states which exercise was seeded and whether it was passed or not — evidence, not a score.

When an AI narrator is configured, a short prose epilogue follows each section's facts — a few mourning lines for the villager, and one for the village when the run ends. With no AI configured, the facts stand complete on their own; and if the narrator ever hiccups, that's just a gap in the prose — the factual record never waits on it. Like the chronicle, the morgue is a generated view of the permanent record underneath: delete it or edit it by hand and the game simply regenerates it, facts intact. When the run ends, your guardian's own final report card (see playing via the guardian) blockquotes into this same section, right beside the narrator's epilogue.

Nothing is ever rewritten

Under all of this sits one append-only history of every single thing that has ever happened in your world — nothing in it can be edited or deleted after the fact. The chronicle and the raw feed are both just readable windows onto that same permanent record, so what you're reading is always the real history, not a reconstruction.

Worth separating, though: that permanent record is not the same thing as any one villager's own memory of it. The history you're reading here never changes, but what a villager personally believes about it can — an untested belief's confidence quietly fades the longer it goes unconfirmed, and once it's faded enough it stops being stated as settled fact and starts showing up, in that villager's own soul, as a hedged "half-remembered" impression instead — see the AI behind the village for how that works. The story stays exact; a villager's grip on it can soften into myth.
