Playing via Metatron

You never touch a villager directly. Everything you do to the village goes through one character: your guardian — the watcher who observes the world and mediates your influence.

You may know this character by its original name, Metatron — the game's own default name for the role is now simply the guardian (a world can even swap in a different name and voice entirely; more on that below). Nothing about how it works changed with the name.

The guardian is your only verb

Your words as a player have exactly one destination: the guardian's own prompt. Villagers never see what you type — they can only ever receive whatever the guardian decides to pass along, and only in the shaped forms described below. This is deliberate: the guardian is a gatekeeper, not a megaphone. You talk to it (in the console, or the guardian pane of the full-screen client — see the screen-orientation page for what that pane shows and the keys reference card for how to focus it), it watches the village, and it decides what — if anything — to do about what you asked.

For an extended back-and-forth, press G (capital) at any moment to open the guardian console — a dedicated, full-screen page for that same conversation, laid out as a readable document rather than the compact pane's scroll: your words and the guardian's replies each get their own labeled block, easier to read back through once a conversation runs long. G, 1, or esc return you to whatever view you had open before; see the keys reference card for the console's other keys.

Shaping your guardian: the charter

The real meta-game is prompt-engineering the guardian itself. At the root of your world's save directory sits charter.md — a plain text file, seeded when the world is created and never overwritten by the game, that you can edit anytime. It's read fresh every single turn, so an edit you make takes effect on the guardian's very next reply. Think of it like giving your guardian a personality and standing instructions: sarcastic, protective, meddling, hands-off — whatever tone and priorities you write in there is who the guardian becomes.

The game also quietly keeps a record of which version of your charter was actually in force, turn by turn — so if a villager dies, the morgue's epitaph can state honestly what your standing instructions were at that moment.

You can also add narrower instruction files in a skills/ folder next to the charter, and control exactly which abilities the guardian is allowed to use — visions, omens, standing orders, workings, and clock control alike — via a capabilities.json file. A world with no such file grants the full set of abilities by default.

The guardian console (press G) puts a live charter/skills panel right beside the conversation, showing at a glance whether your charter is the default, your own writing, or locked to a stage preset, and whether any skill files are bound or still locked. Press e there to open charter.md directly in your own text editor — whatever your $EDITOR is set to; there's no text editor built into the game itself, just this handoff to your own. The moment you save and return, the console confirms "charter changed — next turn binds it," so you know exactly when your edit takes effect.

One caveat if your world sits on the learning ladder (see getting started): at the first stage, The Voice, your guardian's instruction file is fixed — a built-in preset charter guides it no matter what you write, and editing unlocks at The Written Word. The guardian's tool roster grows with your stage too, so at the early stages some of the abilities on this page (workings, skill files, bundles) simply aren't on its menu yet.

Underneath anything you write, two rules always hold no matter what the charter says: the guardian will never invent an event it didn't actually observe, and it will never pass your literal words straight to a villager. Those two guardrails aren't editable — everything else about its voice and judgment is yours to shape. A related rule binds the abilities below: outside of the one survival carve-out described near the bottom of this page, the guardian only acts when you ask it to, or when a standing order you placed authorizes it — never on its own initiative.

Asking for exact facts

You can ask your guardian to explain the game's own mechanics directly — what a working costs, how many charges you have, what each decision verdict means, what a map symbol is, or what any one of its own abilities does. It answers from the game's actual numbers and rules, never a guess: "what does snapping time cost?" or "what does the △ symbol mean?" both get you the real answer, word for word the same one enforcement uses. Asking is free, doesn't spend a charge, and doesn't count as the guardian's one act for the turn — you can ask as many questions as you like before (or instead of) asking for anything to actually happen.

Giving your guardian new tools: bundles

Beyond the charter, you can extend what the guardian can actually do. A bundle is a folder you drop into a bundles/ directory inside your world's save directory — no rebuilding, no code changes to the game itself. Each tool inside a bundle is a small description file that names the tool, explains it, and says what it does in the world (move someone, hand over an item, whisper a memory), optionally with a short script for tools whose behavior should depend on the world — say, a light-conjuring tool that acts differently at night. Restart the world and the new tools simply appear in the guardian's repertoire, priced in the same charge economy as everything else.

A bundle can also carry a whole persona: a SOUL.md file whose text joins the charter in shaping who your guardian is, and its own capabilities.json that can narrow (never expand) what the world already allows. Broken bundles never break your world — at startup each problem is reported plainly in the world's log, naming the file and what's wrong, and everything valid still loads. If a bundle tool shares a name with a built-in ability, the built-in wins. Everything bundles do passes through the same safety valve as the guardian's own workings: only pre-approved kinds of world changes can ever land, and a tool that misbehaves changes nothing at all. The full authoring guide lives in docs/bundles.md in the project repository.

Reaching a villager's mind: visions and omens

When the guardian decides to plant something in a villager's mind — rather than changing the world directly — it has two forms, spent from the same bank of "charges" described below:

A vision — one villager, any time

A vision reaches exactly one living villager, at any hour of the day or night. It lands as a memory the villager will interpret in their own persona — a vision doesn't force a villager to do anything, it just gives them something to react to, like anyone would react to a strange or striking dream. A vision can also carry a place: tell the guardian to show a villager where something actually is — a fire, a shelter, a stand of trees, a chest — and, so long as that place genuinely exists somewhere in the world, the villager now knows exactly where it is, the same as if they'd stumbled across it themselves. It's a way to hand a lost or newly-arrived villager a landmark without walking them there yourself.

An omen — one villager, a named few, or everyone, but only after dark

An omen can reach one villager, a comma-separated handful of named villagers, or the whole village at once ("everyone") — but only at night. If you ask for an omen during the day, the guardian doesn't refuse and doesn't lose the request: it quietly holds onto it and sends the very same omen automatically the moment night falls, to whoever is still alive by then. Either way, landing an omen costs exactly one charge, no matter how many villagers it reaches.

Standing orders: tell it once, and walk away

You can also give the guardian a standing instruction — "when Rowan next falls asleep, send her a comforting vision" — and simply walk away. The guardian keeps watching for that condition for free; it only spends a charge if and when the watched-for moment actually arrives and it acts on your behalf.

  • Up to 3 of your own standing orders can be active at once.
  • Each one expires on its own after a few game days if it never fires (you can choose a shorter or longer window when you place it).
  • You can ask the guardian what orders currently stand, and cancel any of them, any time.
  • When an order fires, the guardian acts through all of its normal rules — the same charter, the same guardrails, the same charge economy as anything you ask for directly — and tells you what it did the next time you open the console, even if that's days later.
  • If the guardian's charges (or its model budget) are exhausted right when an order fires, it doesn't fake the act — it honestly tells you it couldn't follow through, rather than pretending something happened that didn't.

A watch phrased loosely — "when Rowan seems heartbroken," rather than a plain named event — costs the guardian a small, cheap, rate-limited extra check each time something plausibly matches, just to confirm the fuzzy condition is really true before it acts on it. This never draws from your charge bank; only a landed vision, omen, or working does that.

Watching over life and death, on its own

Separately from anything you set up yourself, every world is born with three standing watches the guardian keeps over the village's survival, for free, from the very first tick: one villager slipping toward death, one going hungry enough to starve, and one going cold enough to freeze. You never place these — they exist by the guardian's own nature, and you can't cancel them either. For these three perils alone, the guardian doesn't wait to be asked: it may send a vision or work one of its powers on its own initiative, right then, to try to save the villager in danger — spending a charge exactly as it would for anything you asked for directly. If its charge bank is empty when a life is on the line, it still wakes and tries — you'll see an honest account of a watch that woke to find itself unable to help, never a silent nothing.

That's the one and only exception to "the guardian never acts on its own." Your world's clock and every other standing order remain entirely yours to command — the guardian will still never pause, resume, change speed, or act on a watch you didn't place, without you asking. Saving a life in the moment is the sole carve-out, and it doesn't reach any further than that.

Asking for pause, resume, or a speed change

You can also just ask the guardian in conversation to pause the world, start it back up (at a given speed, if you like), or change its speed — the same controls available from the command line or the full-screen client. This costs nothing, and the guardian will only ever touch the clock when you ask directly, or when a standing order you placed specifically authorizes it — never on its own initiative.

Workings — direct edits to the world

A working (what the game's own mechanics still call a "miracle" under the hood — you'll see that word if you ever poke at the raw event feed) is a concrete, direct change to the world itself, of which there are exactly four kinds:

WorkingWhat it does
Snap timeJump the world's clock forward to a specific time (forward only — you can't rewind)
Give an itemHand a chosen villager some quantity of an item
Move somethingRelocate a villager, a structure, or a pile of goods to a new spot — a moved villager also takes in their new surroundings on arrival, exactly as if they'd walked there, rather than landing blind
Remove somethingDelete a structure, a pile, or a patch of terrain — villagers themselves can never be removed by any working, ever

Workings are memorable too — a time snap feels to every villager like time lurching forward in an instant; a moved villager feels like an unseen hand lifted them somewhere new; a given item just appears in the recipient's hands "as if set there by an unseen giver."

The report card: what your words actually did

From time to time — after a run ends, after a scenario exercise resolves, or during a quiet moment while paused — your guardian writes itself a short report card: two or three sentences tying what it actually did back to your own charter's words, each claim pointing at a real, recorded moment in the log rather than a vague impression. You'll see it in the guardian console, sitting beneath the day-to-day rubric checklist when one exists. It only appears when there's something new to say — it never repeats itself between reports, and if no AI narrator is configured, it simply doesn't show up; the rest of the game works exactly the same either way.

The charge economy

You start with 1 charge, the bank caps at 3, and it regenerates by 1 every 6 game hours. A landed vision or omen costs 1 charge regardless of who it reaches; most workings cost 1 charge too, with snapping time the priciest move at 2 charges. Run out, and the guardian will politely decline and explain why — that refusal costs nothing, so you can always keep talking even with an empty bank. Placing a standing order, and the guardian simply watching for it, are both free; only the eventual landed act spends a charge.

The village's own laws (not the guardian's business)

Separately from anything you do, the village governs itself: once a day the villagers gather at a meeting, air grievances, and vote new rules (like a curfew, or banishing someone) into a standing charter of their own — village_charter.md, distinct from your charter.md. (A villager who's deliberately warming themselves at a fire right then doesn't count toward whether enough people have gathered to hold the meeting — someone recovering from the cold isn't the same as someone choosing to assemble.) Whether a villager actually obeys a law is up to their own persona; the rules aren't force fields, they're social pressure with real, witnessed consequences when broken. You can't vote or propose rules yourself, but the chronicle narrates the assemblies, tallies, and any exiles as they happen, so you always know what your village has decided for itself.
