The AI behind the village

What's actually "thinking" for your villagers, what's just habit, and why some decisions use a fast cheap model while others use a slower, better one.

Nature and soul: what stays fixed, what grows

Every villager starts with a written personality — their nature — set once when the world is created and never changed again for the rest of that villager's life. On top of that fixed nature, each villager accumulates a "soul": a growing record of memories, beliefs, and a sense of who they're becoming, shaped by everything that happens to them. The short version: a villager's souls change over time, but their underlying nature doesn't. That split is enforced, not just a design intention — see below.

Not everything needs an AI call

Plenty of what a villager does — walking somewhere, eating, sleeping, basic reactions — happens instantly through simple built-in rules, no AI model involved at all. Bigger moments — deciding what to do next, how to respond in a conversation, what to say — go through an actual AI model "thinking" as that villager, using their persona and recent memories as the basis for the decision. This mix is why a village can run smoothly at high speed instead of grinding to a halt waiting on AI responses for every little thing: most of a villager's second-to-second existence is free, and AI is spent where it actually matters.

Each villager only knows what they've actually seen

Villagers aren't born knowing the map. Each one carries their own private sense of the world — the ground they've personally walked over, plus a list of things they've actually seen: this fire, that stand of trees, a shelter over there. A villager will only ever act on a place they know about — if nobody in the village has laid eyes on a water's edge yet, nobody goes looking for one to drink from, because as far as any of them know, none exists. Two villagers who have lived very different days genuinely know different things about the same village.

What a villager knows can go stale. A landmark they remember stays "known" for a while even after they've wandered off, but the game quietly treats older memories of changeable things (like a fire, which burns down) as less trustworthy than a memory of somewhere sturdier (like a shelter or a quarry). When a villager actually goes back to a remembered spot and finds it's changed — a fire gone cold, a tree already chopped down — that discovery lands as its own little moment, and it's often memorable enough to make the villager stop and rethink what they were about to do next.

When a villager doesn't know of anything that would help — no known fire to warm up at, no known forage to eat — they can go searching: wander toward the edge of what they've explored so far, taking in whatever's out there as they go. It's a last resort: a hungry villager reaches for it only when they know of no forage and no working den to hunt from, and — since a recent addition — a freezing villager at night reaches for the same last resort when they know of no fire to warm at, are too low on firewood to build one, and know of no tree left to chop for more. Either way it's genuinely nothing-else- to-try territory, not something a well-provisioned villager bothers with.

Directions change hands the same way news and rumors do: when two villagers talk, they'll naturally mention a place or two the other doesn't already know about (or only remembers less clearly) — so a villager who's never left home can still learn where the well is from someone who has. And because a guardian vision can now show a villager exactly where a real place is, you can hand a lost or newly-arrived villager a landmark yourself instead of waiting for them to stumble onto it.

None of this touches how you, the player, watch the village — the full-screen client's map still shows you everything, and the chronicle still narrates the whole village honestly. It's specifically what a villager's own mind has to work with when it's their turn to decide something, and it's why two villagers can react so differently to what looks, from your seat, like the same village.

One thought, one act

When a villager does stop to think, the AI is told three things, plainly: who this villager is (one line, plus their written nature), what their situation looks like right now, and what they remember. Then it must commit to exactly one action for that moment — do something in the world, set a short plan, or just muse. Musing counts: a moment spent thinking is a moment not spent doing, so even daydreaming is a real choice with a real cost, not a free extra. That "one thought, one act" rule is built into the game's machinery, so a villager can never act twice off a single thought. If a model ever hands back an oversized musing (or a plan with too many steps, or a too-long stated reason for what it's doing), the game quietly trims it to the allowed length and keeps it rather than throwing the whole thought away — a verbose model costs a villager some polish, never a wasted turn.

That "what their situation looks like" briefing was recently made more grounded: a villager now also sees their own last few actions and how each turned out — each one honestly labeled with which part of them chose it ("you decided this", "instinct drove this", or "your plan's step"), plus the reason they gave at the time, when they actually gave one — whether each of their needs is rising or falling or holding steady (a felt direction, never a number to obsess over), and the remaining steps of any plan they're partway through, so a villager genuinely remembers what they set out to do. It's all assembled in a fixed order under a strict size budget, with the survival essentials (and their freshest memories) never squeezed out no matter how busy their life gets.

The exact wording villagers are given got a careful rewrite recently — and because this is a game about prompt-writing, the rewrite was held to its own standard: the old and new versions were run head-to-head in identical worlds and only the measurably better one shipped. The new wording produces noticeably fewer garbled decisions, and villagers spend about half as much time lost in thought and more time actually doing things. (A version that also included a worked example was tested too — and rejected, because villagers started copying the example instead of thinking for themselves.)

Picking which memory matters right now (still being tested)

Today, the handful of memories a villager is shown before they think are chosen mostly by how important the memory was and how recent it is — the same "most striking and freshest" rule every time, for everyone. A newer feature, not yet turned on, would instead let the game favor whichever remembered moment actually matches the villager's current situation — so a villager standing at a cold fire would be more likely to be reminded of where a warm one is, even if that's not the single most dramatic thing that ever happened to them. Getting ready for this, every memory and every villager's sense of "what's going on right now" quietly gets a comparable fingerprint behind the scenes already, whether or not the smarter picking is switched on.

It ships off. Before it's ever turned on for real, the plan is to run it quietly alongside the existing behavior first — computing what the smarter pick WOULD have chosen, without changing what a villager actually experiences — and only flip it on once that evidence has been reviewed. So don't expect villagers' recall to feel any different for now; that's the intended, cautious rollout, not something missing or broken. See the setup basics page for the (entirely optional) piece an operator can turn on to start building that evidence.

Local model vs. cloud model

promptworld can split AI work across two kinds of models: a fast, typically free model running on your own computer for high-volume, routine decisions (like the everyday choices villagers make while going about their day), and a slower but higher-quality cloud model reserved for the handful of things worth paying real quality for — most notably nightly memory digestion (below) and the narrated chronicle, plus the guardian's own conversation with you. This split is configurable, and the setup basics page walks through the minimum you need to turn it on. A brand-new world's local model is cogito:3b by default — chosen because it was proven, across long test runs, to reliably follow the game's instructions rather than quietly talking past them.

Because a villager's decisions depend entirely on that local model actually cooperating, promptworld watches its own back: if the configured model turns out to be missing, its server unreachable, or it keeps answering without ever actually following through on an instruction, that's exactly the kind of silent failure that would otherwise just look like villagers stuck endlessly on autopilot (see below) with no explanation. Instead it's surfaced loudly — a warning in promptworld status and a red badge in the full-screen client — and it goes away by itself the moment the problem is fixed, no restart required. See the getting started page for what that looks like.

There's a third, much smaller kind of AI call too: when you've asked the guardian to watch for something loosely phrased ("when Rowan seems heartbroken"), confirming that a matching moment really fits what you described takes one tiny, cheap yes/no check — far smaller than a real conversational reply, and rate-limited so it can never turn watching into meaningful spend. By default it tries the local model first and only asks the cloud model if that isn't available. If you're running an older world whose AI configuration predates this feature, promptworld quietly fills in a sensible default for it the next time the world starts, and says so in one line in the startup log — nothing you need to do by hand.

Why villagers sometimes feel more "on autopilot" at very high speed

An AI model takes real wall-clock time to respond, but the game world keeps ticking during that time. At very high game speeds, a slow decision risks arriving too late to still make sense for the moment it was meant for. Rather than let a stale decision land anyway, the game simply falls back to the simple built-in behavior for that moment instead. You may notice villagers acting a bit more routine-driven at the fastest speeds — that's this trade-off in action, not a bug; dial the speed back down and full AI-driven decisions resume.

You don't have to just infer this is happening from the vibe of your village: promptworld status, and the full-screen client's header badge and systems tab, show it live — for each kind of thinking (planning, conversations, meetings), whether it's currently keeping up or being skipped at your current speed, plus a running skipped N count of how many thoughts that kind has lost since the daemon started. It's a plain, ongoing answer to "how much has going this fast actually cost me," not just a one-time warning when you first change speed — see the time & speed page for where to find it.

Paused doesn't mean unreachable

Pausing freezes the villagers' world, but it doesn't hang up on the guardian: you can keep talking to the guardian while paused, and if you ask for a nudge — a vision or an omen sent to a villager — it still lands. Better than that: the nudged villager wakes up and has exactly one thought about it, right there in the frozen moment, and because no game time is passing, that thought can never arrive "too late" — the skipping described above simply doesn't apply while paused, whatever speed the world is set to return to. This makes pause a natural writing desk: pause, rewrite the guardian's charter, ask for a nudge, and watch how the villager's one thought comes out under your new wording before letting time move again. One nudge buys one thought — nudging the same villager again in the same pause won't produce a second one until time has actually moved on (the same cool-down that stops a running villager from over-thinking applies, and the clock it runs on is frozen). Only the nudged villager stirs; everyone else stays perfectly still, and everything that happens this way is recorded in the world's history like any other moment.

The game keeps relearning how slow its own AI actually is

Behind the "is this decision still worth waiting for" call above, the game keeps a running sense of how long a typical thought actually takes to come back from whatever AI model is answering it. That pace isn't assumed to be fixed forever: if a model starts consistently taking a lot longer than it used to — busier hardware, a different model swapped in, or anything else — the game notices once enough recent thoughts have run well over its expectation, and it updates its own sense of "how long this normally takes" to match what's actually happening now, instead of clinging to an outdated number. That keeps every speed and staleness decision honest — grounded in how the AI is actually performing right now, not how it used to perform a while back. What the game learns this way isn't forgotten when a world stops, either: the learned pace is saved with the world and picked up again on the next start, so restarting doesn't send it back to an over-optimistic guess it has to painfully re-learn.

You can always see where that sense of pace came from: promptworld status lists, per configured AI provider, either the date you last ran promptworld calibrate on it, or uncalibrated (bootstrap) if you never have. A world starts out uncalibrated, running on cautious built-in guesses rather than a measured pace, and says so loudly the first time you start it. Running calibrate is worth doing once your setup is settled — and its own output is honest about its limits too: it measures one AI response at a time, while a real, busy village asks the same model for many things at once, so the number it reports is a floor, not a ceiling — the live world's own sense of pace adapts upward from there as it actually runs.

The world would rather slow down than let a thought go stale

The autopilot fallback above is a last resort, not the first line of defense. Underneath it, the world keeps a running measure of how many villager thoughts are currently in flight and how overdue they're getting for the speed you asked for. A thought that's already running late counts for exactly how late it is — the longer it drags on, the more it weighs — so a village that's genuinely struggling to keep up always gets caught by this and slowed down, rather than slipping through unnoticed. If that overdue-ness climbs too high for a few seconds running, the world quietly drops your effective speed by one notch on its own — giving whatever model is serving your villagers more real time to think per game-second, no input needed from you. Once the backlog clears for a good stretch, it climbs back up again, all the way to the speed you actually asked for. Your own speed and pause commands always cut through this instantly. See the time & speed page for what this looks like in the full-screen client's header and chronicle feed.

The two mechanisms work together, in order: the world tries slowing itself down first, buying villagers more real thinking time; only if you're already down at the slowest speed on the watchable ladder and villagers are still falling behind does the autopilot fallback above kick in.

Teaching worlds pick their speed to protect that thinking

Everything above about a villager's thinking getting suppressed at very high speed is exactly what a teaching world is built to avoid by default. Mark a world as a teaching world (at creation with --teaching, or later with promptworld teaching demo on) and it starts each session at the fastest speed its own measured setup can honestly keep villager thinking running at — worked out from that world's calibration, not a guess or a fixed number. You can still push the speed higher; it isn't blocked, but the game tells you, in the same terms as the horizon lines above, exactly which kinds of thinking (planning, conversations, meetings) that would suppress. An uncalibrated teaching world can't know its own honest number yet, so it asks for promptworld calibrate first. See the time & speed page for the full story.

Sleep is when a soul actually grows

Once a night, when a villager sleeps, one AI call (using the higher-quality cloud model) reviews everything memorable that happened to them that day and folds it into their permanent soul: some memories get promoted as worth keeping long-term, some fade away, new beliefs form (each one noting how sure the villager is, and whether they witnessed it themselves, were told, or simply inferred it), and their short self-narrative — "who I am becoming" — gets updated. Crucially, this process is not allowed to touch the villager's underlying nature: a separate, purely mechanical check throws out any digestion attempt that starts drifting the villager's fundamental temperament. The nightly digest can only grow the soul; it can never rewrite the nature underneath it.

The game doesn't just take a villager's word for "I witnessed this." Every memory quietly carries a record of where it actually came from — something the villager did or saw themselves, versus something they only heard about, inferred, or read as a one-line summary of a longer conversation. When a new belief claims to be "witnessed," the game checks that claim against that record: if it isn't actually backed by something the villager directly experienced, the claim is honestly downgraded to "told" or "inferred" instead, even if the villager's own account overstated it. Nobody in your village can brag about having seen something they only heard secondhand.

Beliefs fade into half-remembered myth if nothing keeps them alive

A belief doesn't stay at full strength forever just because a villager once formed it. Its confidence quietly fades the longer it goes unconfirmed — roughly halving every eight game days, far slower than an ordinary memory (which fades within about a day), so a real conviction still outlasts a fleeting impression. Once a belief's confidence has faded far enough, it stops steering how that villager behaves, and you'll see it rendered in their soul not as a stated fact but as a hedged "half-remembered: ..." line instead. Nothing needs to happen for this to occur — it's simply what happens to an old belief nobody has re-confirmed. A belief a villager keeps re-experiencing or re-confirming, on the other hand, stays fresh and never fades.

Relationships, gossip, and grudges

Villagers keep track of how they feel about each other — trust and fondness that rise and fall based on what actually happens between them: talking, giving gifts, breaking a promise, or taking from someone's storage chest without asking (which is noticed and remembered, and damages the relationship, even though the goods still change hands). Rumors spread from villager to villager, getting slightly reworded each time they're retold, and a villager's confidence in a rumor fades the more times it's been passed along — exactly how gossip behaves in real life. None of this is decoration: real grudges and real friendships form from it, and you can read the results — arguments, favors, broken promises — right in the chronicle.

Two villagers won't spin up a full back-and-forth exchange again immediately after they last spoke — one of them needs something new to have actually happened since then. It's a small guard against a pair looping the same conversation over and over; a brief, wordless exchange still happens either way, it just doesn't always blossom into the fuller scene you'd see rendered in the chronicle.
