1  What you’re getting into

You don’t play a character. You have eight villagers, each with a mind of its own, and one intermediary who will do things on your behalf if you ask well. That’s the whole game.

The other thing to understand before anything else is that the world is not a program you run. It’s a program that is already running.

!
Warning

A villager who dies stays dead. There is no reload and no undo. Your first village will probably lose someone.

§
Design intent

Losing a villager is the story, not a failure state. The game narrates a death the same way it narrates a harvest, and the postmortem — what led up to it, who was there, what changed after — is the payoff you came for.

2  How you actually do anything

You cannot click a villager and tell it to chop wood. Every bit of influence you have goes through one intermediary — the Guardian — and it goes through in words.

×
Pitfall

New players tell the Guardian what they want to be true (“keep everyone warm”). Tell it what to watch for and what to do about it instead — that’s the whole skill the game is teaching.

Tip

4  Before you start

  1. Go installed. That’s the only hard requirement.
  2. A local model server — optional, but it’s the difference between villagers who react and villagers who think.
  3. Twenty minutes, and a willingness to watch for ten of them without touching anything.
i
Note

With no model configured the world still runs — villagers walk, eat, sleep and freeze on reflex alone. It’s a real game and worth an hour. Chapter 1 covers turning minds on.

Under the hood — why speed is a ceiling, not a promise

Villager decisions cost a model call, and model calls take real time. When you raise the speed, the game raises the ceiling; if thinking can’t keep up, it quietly settles lower rather than letting villagers stop thinking. Chapter 2 covers what you’ll see when that happens.

Under the hood — collapsed by default

Optional mechanical depth. Never load-bearing: a reader who skips every one of these still finishes the guide able to play.

§
Design intent

Why the game refuses to do the thing you want. Used sparingly — roughly once per chapter, at the point of maximum frustration.

Rules the prose has to follow

  • No paragraph over four rendered lines. Longer becomes a list or a table.
  • Every procedure is a numbered list, one action per step, imperative mood.
  • Every “what does this mean” answer is a table.
  • At least one callout per <h2> section — they are the main defence against wall-of-text.
  • A page splits at 8,000 words, at the nearest <h2>. A contents list over ~12 entries is the earlier warning.
