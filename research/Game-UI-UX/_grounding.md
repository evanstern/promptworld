# Dwarf Fortress UI/UX — Cited Fact Digest

## 1. The classic (pre-Steam) keyboard-driven ASCII interface

- Classic Dwarf Fortress renders its world entirely in text-based graphics — letters, numbers, and box-drawing characters in sixteen colors; a dwarf is drawn as `☺`, a cat as `c`, a dog as `d` ([Wikipedia — Dwarf Fortress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dwarf_Fortress)).
- The interface is driven by single-keypress top-level menus: `d` opens designations (dig/chop/gather orders painted onto the map), `b` opens building, `k` enters "look around" mode, `u` opens the units list, and `v` views individual creatures. The wiki notes these keys' "function can change depending on what game mode you are using and what screen you are at," and menu-specific controls are usually listed at the bottom of each screen ([DF2014:Controls — Dwarf Fortress Wiki](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/DF2014:Controls)).
- List/menu scrolling in classic DF uses `+` and `-` to move to the next/previous option and `*` and `/` to move between pages — because "when it is possible to scroll the map while a menu is open, the arrow keys cannot be used to select menu options; instead, the +, -, *, and / keys are repurposed to allow selection from menus" ([DF2014:Controls — Dwarf Fortress Wiki](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/DF2014:Controls)). Community discussion of the classic UI describes "at least three different ways of scrolling" across different menus as one of the notorious inconsistencies ([Steam discussions](https://steamcommunity.com/app/975370/discussions/0/3416557114751453595)).
- The map is a 3D volume viewed one horizontal slice at a time: vertical navigation between z-levels uses `<` (up) and `>` (down), with alternate bindings Shift+5 / Ctrl+5. Cursor movement uses the numpad (7-8-9 / 4-6 / 1-2-3) or arrow keys, with Shift held for fast movement ([DF2014:Controls — Dwarf Fortress Wiki](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/DF2014:Controls)).
- Keybindings are customizable via the Esc menu or by editing `interface.txt`, though the wiki advises this "is not recommended for new players" ([DF2014:Controls — Dwarf Fortress Wiki](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/DF2014:Controls)).
- Learning-curve criticism is extensively documented: Ars Technica's Casey Johnston wrote that performing basic actions was like "trying to build a skyscraper by banging two rocks together," and noted players had "no autonomy even after hours" of play, with no in-game tutorial; Rock Paper Shotgun's Quintin Smith wrote that "getting it to do what you want is like teaching a beetle to cook"; multiple reviewers described the game as "unusually difficult to learn" ([Wikipedia — Dwarf Fortress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dwarf_Fortress)).
- The classic engine is a single C/C++ codebase — Tarn Adams counted "711,000 lines" in late 2021 ("Searching for ';', which is a loose method but close enough, we're up to 711,000 lines") — built on OpenGL and SDL with FMOD for sound, developed essentially by one programmer for 20 years ([Stack Overflow blog, Dec 2021](https://stackoverflow.blog/2021/12/31/700000-lines-of-code-20-years-and-one-developer-how-dwarf-fortress-is-built/)).

## 2. The 2022 Steam "Premium" release and its rationale

- Announced in March 2019; released December 6, 2022 on Steam and itch.io at $29.99, published by Kitfox Games. Adams cited "family situations" and "waning income from Patreon" as the motivation to monetize; his brother Zach's health scare was the trigger — Tarn: "If the same thing happened to my brother, we'd be wiped out, Bay 12 would not exist" ([Wikipedia — Dwarf Fortress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dwarf_Fortress)); [Game Rant interview](https://gamerant.com/dwarf-fortress-interview-new-age-health-problems-inspiration/); [Kitfox launch devlog](https://kitfoxgames.itch.io/dwarf-fortress/devlog/460421/dwarf-fortress-is-out-now)).
- On why the classic UI stayed unfinished for over a decade, Tarn Adams told PC Gamer in 2019: "there were always new features to work on, and the game was perpetually unfinished anyway; why work on usability when those menus would surely be changing in the future, right?" — and PC Gamer reported the brothers had known the "obtuse layers of keyboard commands" were a barrier "the entire time," with the Steam release being the point where Adams said it was finally time to focus on usability features like mouse support ([PC Gamer, 2019](https://www.pcgamer.com/tutorials-and-mouse-support-could-make-dwarf-fortress-on-steam-vastly-easier-to-play/)).
- What shipped at launch: a new pixel-art tileset, an interactive tutorial, a "new user interface and navigable menus," full mouse functionality, a new soundtrack (with tracks sung in the constructed Dwarvish language, composed by Dabu, Simon Swerwer, and Águeda Macias), Steam Workshop mod support, and quality-of-life additions; only Fortress and Legends modes at launch, with Adventure and Arena modes to follow ([Kitfox launch devlog](https://kitfoxgames.itch.io/dwarf-fortress/devlog/460421/dwarf-fortress-is-out-now)).
- The premium tileset was made by two long-time community tileset modders hired for the job: Michał "Mayday" Madej and Patrick Martin "Meph" Schroeder ([Variety, 2019](https://variety.com/2019/gaming/news/dwarf-fortress-steam-itchio-1203162351/)).
- Who did the UI work — Tarn Adams: "The new UI [was] mostly Zach and I and to some extent also Mike and Meph, as we were working through art stuff early on and menus were a constant topic." He added they "had an actual UX person take a look later on (later than we should have, not a mistake we're planning to make with adventure mode), which is why we have our nice building menu" ([Game Developer interview](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/programming/how-tarn-adams-upgraded-and-optimized-dwarf-fortress-for-its-official-steam-release)).
- Classic and Premium are not separate programs — Adams: "There aren't really two codebases at all, haha. The switch that lets you go between Classic and graphical is just swapping out some glyphs. The grid structure underneath is the same everywhere" ([Game Developer interview](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/programming/how-tarn-adams-upgraded-and-optimized-dwarf-fortress-for-its-official-steam-release)).
- Bay 12 hired its first additional programmer, community member Putnam, after release — Adams: "We've brought on Putnam, the new programmer, as of this year, and there haven't been any troubles yet"; a community manager was also brought on ([Game Developer interview](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/programming/how-tarn-adams-upgraded-and-optimized-dwarf-fortress-for-its-official-steam-release); [Wikipedia — Dwarf Fortress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dwarf_Fortress)).
- Pre-release previews described the concrete menu changes: old menus that "look like classic DOS file system readouts" replaced by menus that are "still a bit information-dense but now have scrollbars and clickable tabs and buttons," screens that scale to widescreen monitors, and an accounting screen organized into Dwarves / Items / Animals tabs with pixel-art item icons ([PCGamesN](https://www.pcgamesn.com/dwarf-fortress/menus)).
- The free ASCII classic version remained available on Bay 12's site, and the mouse/UI improvements were stated to flow back into it; the options menu in Premium can switch between new and old visuals ([Kitfox launch devlog](https://kitfoxgames.itch.io/dwarf-fortress/devlog/460421/dwarf-fortress-is-out-now); [Wikipedia — Dwarf Fortress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dwarf_Fortress)).
- Adams on the result: "It's so much easier now than before. I would never go back" ([Game Rant interview](https://gamerant.com/dwarf-fortress-interview-new-age-health-problems-inspiration/)).

## 3. Documented reception: what the redesign fixed and broke

- Critical reception of the Steam release: Metacritic 93/100, IGN 10/10, The Guardian 5/5; it was the best-selling title on Steam upon release ([Wikipedia — Dwarf Fortress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dwarf_Fortress)).
- Sales/adoption facts: pre-release projections of ~160,000 units in two months were exceeded within the first day; ~300,000 copies sold in the first week; over 500,000 by end of 2022; 600,000+ by March 2023; 800,000+ within the first year; over 1 million by April 2025. January 2023 revenue exceeded $7.2 million, versus roughly $15,000/month in donations pre-Steam ([Wikipedia — Dwarf Fortress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dwarf_Fortress)).
- PC Gamer's review praised native mouse support ("clicking to designate/interact with/inspect things" called a much-needed change) but argued the classic keyboard interface "followed a reliable logic with clean separation between playspace and menu information, while the new interface feels scattered in comparison," noted the "level of visual noise can get overwhelming... in a busier fortress," and flagged that mouse support "came at the cost of ripping out shortcuts used to navigate menus," a concern for RSI-prone players ([PC Gamer review](https://www.pcgamer.com/dwarf-fortress-review/)).
- DSOGaming's Premium review called the new UI "incredibly cumbersome and a chore to get through," citing menus that reset scroll position ("you'll need to scroll through the entire list again... each and every time"), no batch operations, and no hyperlinking between related records outside Legends mode; it called the tutorial "incredibly barebones," saying players still must consult wikis and video guides ([DSOGaming review](https://www.dsogaming.com/special/reviews/dwarf-fortress-premium-pc-review/)).
- Steam community threads document that veterans of classic found the keyboard-to-mouse transition disorienting — v50 is mouse-first with keyboard shortcuts "not the same as before" — while agreeing the classic UI's "confusing menu differences and at least three different ways of scrolling" were real defects the redesign removed ([Steam discussions](https://steamcommunity.com/app/975370/discussions/0/3416557114751453595)).

## 4. Information-density patterns: presenting huge simulation state

- Announcements: messages appear at the top-left of the screen; "major announcements" pause gameplay and center the camera on the event in question. The full announcements list (`N` in Premium, `a`/reports in classic) lists all announcements in dated order, and some (but not all) entries can be zoomed to ([DF2014:Announcement — Dwarf Fortress Wiki](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/DF2014:Announcement)).
- The `k` "look" cursor: an X-shaped cursor moved tile-by-tile over the map; the side panel lists everything in the selected tile in a fixed hierarchy — creatures, then objects, then buildings, then map tile info ([DF2014:Controls guide — Dwarf Fortress Wiki](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/DF2014:Controls_guide)).
- "Zoom to creature" from lists (e.g., the units screen) drops the player into View Creature mode with the cursor centered on the selected unit — the standard pattern for jumping from list-state to map-state ([DF2014:Controls guide — Dwarf Fortress Wiki](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/DF2014:Controls_guide)).
- Per-dwarf simulation state exposed through text screens: Adams described in interviews that individual dwarves have "emotional states, favorite gems, and grudges," all surfaced through the text-based thoughts/relationships screens ([Stack Overflow blog](https://stackoverflow.blog/2021/12/31/700000-lines-of-code-20-years-and-one-developer-how-dwarf-fortress-is-built/)).
- In Premium, the redesigned information screens consolidate fortress state into tabbed, mouse-scrollable panels (the Dwarves/Items/Animals accounting screen); previews characterized even the new menus as "still a bit information-dense" ([PCGamesN](https://www.pcgamesn.com/dwarf-fortress/menus)).
- The wiki maintains a curated list of dozens of developer interviews and talks (including GDC and podcast appearances by Tarn and Zach Adams) documenting design rationale over 20 years ([List of Dwarf Fortress developer interviews](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/List_of_Dwarf_Fortress_developer_interviews)).

## 5. The community UX layer: what third-party tools reveal about the native UI

- **Dwarf Therapist** (first released 2009 by Trey Stout, then maintained by DwarfEngineer 2010–2012, Splinterz from 2013, Clément since 2017) attaches to a running DF process and reads game memory to provide "an advanced GUI to manage and check dwarf job allocations, military assignments, statistics (such as attributes, personality traits, happiness, and health)," with sorting by profession, migration wave, happiness, etc., plus a semi-automatic "labor optimizer." Changes are committed back into the game. The wiki's pull-quote: "It makes Dwarf fortress playable. It's that good." It remains "fully compatible with v50+ premium and classic" ([Utility:Dwarf therapist — Dwarf Fortress Wiki](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/Utility:Dwarf_therapist)). The native labor UI it bypasses is documented on the wiki's labor page as difficult enough that third-party tools are the standard recommendation ([DF2014:Labor — Dwarf Fortress Wiki](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/DF2014:Labor)).
- **DFHack** is "a Dwarf Fortress memory access library, distributed with a wide variety of useful scripts and plugins," shipping "a significant suite of bugfixes and interface enhancements by default" plus automation tools (e.g., autofarm) for "aspects of gameplay many players find toilsome," accessed through an in-game console ([DFHack Introduction](https://docs.dfhack.org/en/stable/docs/Introduction.html)). Its `manipulator` plugin is explicitly documented as "an in-game equivalent to the popular program Dwarf Therapist" — a labor grid embedded into the native units screen ([DFHack manipulator docs](https://docs.dfhack.org/en/stable/docs/tools/manipulator.html)); its `labormanager` plugin auto-assigns "best fit" dwarves to needed labors ([DFHack labormanager docs](https://docs.dfhack.org/en/stable/docs/tools/labormanager.html)).
- **Tilesets and visualizers:** community tileset packs by Mayday and Meph were popular enough that Bay 12/Kitfox hired both authors to make the official Premium tileset ([Variety](https://variety.com/2019/gaming/news/dwarf-fortress-steam-itchio-1203162351/)); Stonesense (built on DFHack) renders the game in 3D isometric view, and Adams publicly endorsed third-party tools despite the game being closed-source, stating he admires the third-party developers and would "never implement" modern 3D graphics himself ([Wikipedia — Dwarf Fortress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dwarf_Fortress)).

# RimWorld UI/UX — Cited Fact Digest

## 1. Core UI architecture

**Overall screen layout.** The official wiki's [User interface page](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/User_interface) maps the main game screen ("Colony Map") as a fixed set of regions: the Architect menu and inspect pane in the bottom-left corner; a transparent resources list in the top-left; the menu tab row along the bottom; toggle switches, time-speed control, and the current date in the bottom-right; alerts and suggestions in the top-right; the learning helper at top-right; and the colonist bar across the top ([RimWorld Wiki: User interface](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/User_interface)). When neither the Architect menu nor the inspect pane is open, the same bottom-left area shows "cell info" — roofing, terrain, objects, filth, and the cell's light level under the cursor ([RimWorld Wiki: User interface](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/User_interface)).

**Architect menu.** The Architect menu "is used to designate construction blueprints, give orders to colonists, and create new zones/areas" and is described by the wiki as "the most used menu in the game" ([RimWorld Wiki: Architect](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Architect)). Its submenus are organized as tabs: Orders (deconstruct, mine, haul, cut plants, hunt, tame, plus a "plan" tool for drawing layouts without spending resources), Zone/Area, Structure, Production, Furniture, Power, Security, Misc, Floors, Recreation, Ship, Temperature — and DLC-specific tabs (Ideology, Biotech, Anomaly, Odyssey) each containing that expansion's buildings ([RimWorld Wiki: Architect](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Architect)). Ideology's buildings were moved into their own architect tab in update 1.4 ([RimWorld 1.4 public changelog](https://docs.google.com/document/u/1/d/e/2PACX-1vRVirkmrIX9ladhT8FTEKtOA_JZ1dP8IK5ZaHL8zfd2pOOVm5xyPcO6JUKoNZeNt7RWg33vBx0uNqLP/pub)).

**Bottom menu row.** The bottom-edge menus are Architect, Work, Schedule, Assign, Animals, Wildlife, Research, Quests, World, History, Factions, Menu (plus Ideology with that DLC); "the first eight menus are bound to hotkeys F1 through F8" ([RimWorld Wiki: Menus](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Menus)). The Research icon in the menu bar doubles as a progress bar for the current research project; in the research tree, "green boxes are completed research, black boxes are unfinished research, and gray boxes are unavailable research" ([RimWorld Wiki: Menus](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Menus)).

**Work priorities grid.** The Work menu (default F1) is a colonists-by-work-types grid with two modes toggled by "Manual priorities": standard mode, where colonists do assigned work types (green check) with tasks ordered left-to-right by importance; and manual mode, where each cell takes a numeric priority from 1 (highest) to 4 (lowest), blank meaning never ([RimWorld Wiki: Work](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Work)). "Regardless of mode, all available work of one priority will be done before the next priority," which the wiki illustrates with a Hauling-at-1 colonist hauling "every single object, even those halfway across the map" first ([RimWorld Wiki: Work](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Work)). Skill is encoded in the cell border: "each box has a visible outline. The higher the colonist's skill, the brighter the outline, ranging from red (worst), to white, to bright yellow (best)"; passion is shown as one or two flame icons in the box; hovering shows the numeric skill level; and "a crunching sound is made when you assign a pawn to a work type which they won't do well due to low skill" ([RimWorld Wiki: Work](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Work)). Pawns incapable of a work type (from backstory, traits, title, etc.) simply have no box for it ([RimWorld Wiki: Work](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Work)). Direct control is via right-click: select a colonist, right-click a target, choose a context-menu option; shift-click chains a job queue that is displayed on the colonist's inspect pane ([RimWorld Wiki: Work](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Work)).

**Colonist bar.** The top-of-screen colonist bar "shows the colonists' names, appearance, and status icons," grouped by settlement and/or caravan; left-click jumps to a colonist on the local map, right-click centers the world map on them, and the bar can be toggled off via a bottom-right switch ([RimWorld Wiki: User interface](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/User_interface)). Update 1.5 added "an option to make colonists' moods on the colonist bar much more visible. They change color and glow when below mental break thresholds," made new pawns always appear at the right end of the bar, and allowed targeting via the colonist bar ([RimWorld 1.5 public changelog](https://docs.google.com/document/d/e/2PACX-1vSgQmFRFk0FATWTjyZkKRq4oa58sQps4D0kE_uoyKR1y3ZXJT1nIMZSsno7T8cfG-Y6B8lVL3QFnbwQ/pub)).

**Inspect pane.** "The inspect pane appears whenever the player selects a pawn, item, or structure, and shows details about that object. If multiple objects of the same type are selected the inspect pane will automatically list the quantity selected" ([RimWorld Wiki: User interface](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/User_interface)). For workstations, the Bills tab in the inspect pane sets production orders: "select the table and open the Bills tab. Here, you can say what meals to cook, what things to craft, what creatures to butcher" ([RimWorld Wiki: Learning helper](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Learning_helper)).

**Time controls.** Time passes in ticks — 60 ticks per real second at normal speed, always 2,500 ticks per in-game hour ([RimWorld Wiki: Time](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Time)). Speed controls sit at the lower right: Pause (Space, x0), Speed 1 (key 1, x1), Speed 2 (key 2, x3), Speed 3 (key 3, x6), and dev-mode-only Speed 4 (key 4, x15, keybind only); the wiki notes the community's "2x/3x/4x" naming "is not representative of the actual speed," and that hardware may prevent the game from actually hitting the target multiplier ([RimWorld Wiki: Time](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Time); [RimWorld Wiki: Controls](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Controls)). The in-game clock/date in the corner reflects a 60-day year of four 15-day "quadrums"; hovering the date lists local seasons ([RimWorld Wiki: Time](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Time)). 1.5 added a 12/24-hour clock option ([1.5 changelog](https://docs.google.com/document/d/e/2PACX-1vSgQmFRFk0FATWTjyZkKRq4oa58sQps4D0kE_uoyKR1y3ZXJT1nIMZSsno7T8cfG-Y6B8lVL3QFnbwQ/pub)).

**Zones and areas.** Zones (stockpile, dumping stockpile, growing) "aren't structures, rather just marked sections of the map for organization and control"; they are always contiguous, cannot overlap, and are configured by selecting the zone and clicking a button above the Architect menu, where stockpile priority is also set; storage settings can be copied between stockpiles, graves, sarcophagi, and shelves ([RimWorld Wiki: Zone](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Zone)). Allowed areas restrict pawn movement: up to eight can be created (plus home area), assigned per-colonist via the Schedule tab's area column, and painted onto the map with Expand/Clear Allowed Area designators in Architect ([RimWorld Wiki: Menus](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Menus); [RimWorld Wiki: Learning helper](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Learning_helper)). Bottom-right toggle switches control visibility of zones, beauty display, home area expansion, roofed-area overlay, and categorized resource list mode ([RimWorld Wiki: User interface](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/User_interface)).

**Schedule grid.** The Schedule screen assigns an activity (Anything/Work/Recreation/Sleep/Meditate) per colonist per hour of a 24-hour day; each setting has documented numeric need thresholds — e.g. under "Anything" a pawn works unless recreation < 35%, food < 30%, or rest < 30% ([RimWorld Wiki: Menus](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Menus)).

## 2. Alerts, letters, and notifications

**Letters (right edge).** "Most events that occur create a Letter, an envelope icon on the right side of the screen… Usually, the color of the envelope will suggest the severity of the event; blue envelopes are good events, grey is neutral, yellow is bad, and red denotes direct threats"; other events pause the game and pop up a choice dialog ([RimWorld Wiki: Events](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Events)). Letter titles are variable and "can mention location, pawn and faction names, specific buffs or debuffs, or even have countdowns for the time variable events" ([RimWorld Wiki: Events](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Events)).

**Alert stack.** Persistent alerts appear at the right/top-right edge: "Alerts when colonists are about to go insane, or if they need rescue. Also displays helpful tips" ([RimWorld Wiki: User interface](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/User_interface)). The game's own learning-helper lesson for alerts reads: "The alerts at the right edge of the screen tell you about issue that need your attention. Keep an eye on the alerts" ([RimWorld Wiki: Learning helper](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Learning_helper)). The wiki's Messages examples include "Critical alert: Colonist needs rescue," "Critical alert: Fire," and "Critical alert: Major break risk" ([RimWorld Wiki: Menus](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Menus)).

**Click-to-jump.** The learning helper's "Clicking messages" lesson documents jump-to-source: "You can click on the notifications in the top-left of the screen to instantly center the camera on their subject" ([RimWorld Wiki: Learning helper](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Learning_helper)). The same pattern exists in list windows: in the Animals window "clicking an animal name will center the map to that animal," and in Wildlife "mousing over an animal will point an arrow towards it, and clicking will jump the camera to it" ([RimWorld Wiki: Menus](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Menus)).

**History and message persistence.** The History screen's Messages tab "shows the last 200 messages and letters you've received, even after you close them. You can pin messages to keep them around longer" ([RimWorld Wiki: Menus](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Menus)). The History graph tab (wealth, population, average colonist mood) uses the same severity color language: "Blue or white circles are positive or neutral events, yellow circles are negative events, and red circles are attacks," plotted chronologically on the graph and filterable to the last 300/100/30 days ([RimWorld Wiki: Menus](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Menus)). Update 1.4 made duplicate messages highlight the original instead of stacking new ones and bundled letters together when they can't all fit on screen; 1.5 added right-click dismissal of messages ([1.4 changelog](https://docs.google.com/document/u/1/d/e/2PACX-1vRVirkmrIX9ladhT8FTEKtOA_JZ1dP8IK5ZaHL8zfd2pOOVm5xyPcO6JUKoNZeNt7RWg33vBx0uNqLP/pub); [1.5 changelog](https://docs.google.com/document/d/e/2PACX-1vSgQmFRFk0FATWTjyZkKRq4oa58sQps4D0kE_uoyKR1y3ZXJT1nIMZSsno7T8cfG-Y6B8lVL3QFnbwQ/pub)).

## 3. How RimWorld teaches itself

**Alpha 15 (Aug 2016) introduced both the tutorial and the learning helper.** Tynan Sylvester's release post announces "a rich tutorial, an intelligent learning helper to help you pick up the game"; the tutorial "teaches the basics of setting up a game, building a small self-sustaining colony, and defeating the first raider," and — notably — "locks out irrelevant controls and highlights relevant controls," handing off to a normal storyteller at the end ([Ludeon blog: Alpha 15 – Tutorial and Drugs released!](https://ludeon.com/blog/2016/08/alpha-15-tutorial-and-drugs-released/)).

**Learning helper mechanics (Tynan's own changelog text):** "Learning helper sits in the top right of the screen. If something happens relating to a concept the player hasn't learned, that lesson will be activated and shown on the learning helper… Lessons are automatically marked as learned when the player does the necessary interaction, and can be marked as learned manually. Lessons are shown as needed by circumstance, or on a slow timer. The overall idea is that you'll never be shown a lesson you already know, but always be shown the lessons you need to know now." It can also be "expanded and searched for any lesson, so you can look up how to do a specific thing at any time" ([Ludeon blog: Alpha 15](https://ludeon.com/blog/2016/08/alpha-15-tutorial-and-drugs-released/)). It replaced the earlier "yellow pop-up messages and yellow learning alerts," can be disabled in settings, auto-hides when empty via a bottom-right toggle, and mods can add custom lessons ([RimWorld Wiki: Learning helper](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Learning_helper); [RimWorld Wiki: User interface](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/User_interface)).

**Tynan Sylvester's design writing.** In *Designing Games* (O'Reilly, 2013), Sylvester defines elegance as maximizing "emotional power and variety of play experiences while minimizing player comprehension burden and developer effort," and his Interface-chapter framework treats "noise" as "signal that fails to transmit meaningful information — when players are overwhelmed or confused, signals degrade into noise," with games needing a consistent "metaphor vocabulary" of cues marking which elements are mechanically relevant ([Designing Games summary](https://howtoes.blog/2025/06/12/designing-games-complete-book-summary-all-key-ideas/); [O'Reilly: Designing Games](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/designing-games/9781449338015/)). Directly quoted from the book: "Game designers don't design events. We design systems of mechanics that generate events," and "pursuing that truth makes your designs smaller, simpler, more focused, and more elegant" ([Goodreads: Designing Games quotes](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/21977458-designing-games-a-guide-to-engineering-experiences)). On RimWorld vs. Dwarf Fortress specifically, Sylvester said: "Dwarf Fortress' abstract presentation leaves the player's mind to imagine the story from scratch, the same way your imagination paints a picture from words in a novel… RimWorld is also abstract, but it shows enough that it constrains the imagination a little bit," and that he wanted to push DF's story potential "in a way that's approachable, and without falling into some of the design traps that have, in my opinion, marred Dwarf Fortress" ([Game Developer: How RimWorld fleshes out the Dwarf Fortress formula](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/how-i-rimworld-i-fleshes-out-the-i-dwarf-fortress-i-formula), Aug 2016). His 2017 GDC talk frames RimWorld "not as a game, but a story generator," arguing simple graphics aid narrative because players' apophenia fills gaps, and his theory was a simulation "not overly complex, that a player can observe and comprehend" ([GDC Vault: RimWorld — Contrarian, Ridiculous, and Impossible Game Design Methods](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1024232/-RimWorld-Contrarian-Ridiculous-and); [GDC talk video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VdqhHKjepiE)).

## 4. Pawn information design

**Needs tab / mood bar.** Mood is "a blue bar in the Needs tab ranging from 0 to 100%"; a white triangle "Mood Target" below the bar represents the instantaneous sum of all thoughts, while the bar itself chases the target at a maximum of +12 per in-game hour rising and −8 falling ([RimWorld Wiki: Mood](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Mood)). Three dividing lines on the mood bar mark mental-break thresholds — 5% extreme, 20% major, 35% minor for a normal colonist, modifiable by traits ([RimWorld Wiki: Mood](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Mood)). Base mood is difficulty-dependent (42 on Peaceful down to 22 on "Losing is Fun") ([RimWorld Wiki: Mood](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Mood)). The wiki's Needs page documents the full meter grammar: red/green triangles show each meter's current tendency; white triangles show where a level will settle "if the current situation of the pawn does not change"; black hatch marks mark thresholds where a new thought (e.g. "Unsightly environment," −5 mood) will fire; food/rest/recreation bars have no markers because they "are naturally falling towards zero… like fuel meters"; and mousing over any meter yields a detailed tooltip ([RimWorld Wiki: Needs](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Needs)). The Needs page even carries a section titled "This is confusing, I still don't understand…" explaining that "the meter levels display the long term situation of the pawn, while the markers show you the immediate circumstances" ([RimWorld Wiki: Needs](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Needs)).

**Thoughts with numeric modifiers.** Every mood influence is an itemized "thought" with an explicit signed number and metadata (expiration time in days, stack limit, stacking multiplier) — e.g. "Low expectations +12," "High expectations +6," hunger −6 ([RimWorld Wiki: Thoughts](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Thoughts); [RimWorld Wiki: Mood](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Mood)).

**Health tab.** Each pawn's Health tab has two sub-tabs: an "Overview" (including medical-care preference and food restriction) and an "Operations" tab for scheduling surgery; injuries are tracked per body part against named capacity systems (Sight, Hearing, Moving, Manipulation, Talking, Eating, Breathing, Blood Filtration, Blood Pumping, Digestion, Consciousness), with pain computed by formula and thresholds ([RimWorld Wiki: Health](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Health)). The health tab was redesigned in update 1.5, along with the prisoner tab ([1.5 changelog](https://docs.google.com/document/d/e/2PACX-1vSgQmFRFk0FATWTjyZkKRq4oa58sQps4D0kE_uoyKR1y3ZXJT1nIMZSsno7T8cfG-Y6B8lVL3QFnbwQ/pub)).

## 5. Reception, comparisons, criticisms, and the mod layer

**"Dwarf Fortress made accessible" framing.** Wikipedia records that RimWorld was built in Unity, revealed September 2013, Kickstarted for $268,132, and "favorably compared to other management-survival games, including Dwarf Fortress" ([Wikipedia: RimWorld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RimWorld)). Rock Paper Shotgun's 2016 early-access review positioned it as "the game for everyone who wanted to get into Dwarf Fortress but couldn't because ASCII," while noting it still had its own confusing menus ([RPS early-access review, mirrored](https://bliss.isa-geek.net/2016/07/18/rimworld-review-early-access/)). Brendan Caldwell's RPS 1.0 review (Oct 2018) conceded "it can be confounding at first, not to mention the ugliness of those grey boxes," before praising it as "a management game that feels like you're in charge of people – beautiful, flawed people" ([OpenCritic: Brendan Caldwell reviews](https://opencritic.com/critic/1592/brendan-caldwell?sort=score&page=3)). Comparative analyses credit RimWorld's "clean top-down 2D art style… and intuitive color coding, where every object and pawn is recognizable instantly," versus Dwarf Fortress's steep curve even in its Steam version ([Gamepressure: Dwarf Fortress vs RimWorld](https://www.gamepressure.com/newsroom/dwarf-fortress-vs-rimworld-detailed-comparison/z04ddd)).

**Documented criticisms.** PC Gamer's review called the UI "a bit lacking" and "far from intuitive and full of irksome inconsistencies like being able to mass select some objects but not others" ([PC Gamer: RimWorld review](https://www.pcgamer.com/rimworld-review/)). Community threads document late-game management friction ([Steam discussion: "The Management Tab is Mind-Numblingly Frustrating"](https://steamcommunity.com/app/294100/discussions/0/3880473533201289006/)).

**QoL mods and the gaps they fill (documented feature lists):**
- [Dubs Mint Menus](https://steamcommunity.com/sharedfiles/filedetails/?id=1446523594) — searchable list boxes for recipe bills, health bills, plant selection, architect, and research, "making it easier to find things when lots of mods are running," plus a radial "Mint wheel" designator selector ([GitHub: Dubs-Mint-Menus](https://github.com/Dubwise56/Dubs-Mint-Menus)) — i.e., search where vanilla long lacked it.
- [RimHUD](https://github.com/Jaxe-Dev/RimHUD) — "adds more information to the RimWorld UI": a detailed HUD integrated into (or floating beside) the inspect pane, with "visual warnings… if a pawn has any life-threatening conditions, wounds that need tending to, or potentially unstable mental states."
- [Interaction Bubbles](https://rimworldbase.com/interaction-bubbles-mod/) — surfaces social-interaction text "that would normally only be found in the log" as speech bubbles over pawns.
- [Colony Manager](https://steamcommunity.com/sharedfiles/filedetails/?id=715565262) — a Manager tab automating hunting/forestry/livestock job creation, addressing repetitive late-game order-issuing.
- Work-tab mods like [Drag and Drop Work Tab / DDWorkTab](https://github.com/DingoDjango/DDWorkTab) add drag-prioritization and mass enable/disable — gaps in the vanilla 1–4 grid at scale.
- [Command Palette](https://steamcommunity.com/app/294100/discussions/0/3808408328761801781/) is cited in comparisons as a searchable menu over all construction/zone orders.

The teaching layer arrived in Alpha 15 (2016) as above; 1.0 shipped October 17, 2018 ([Wikipedia: RimWorld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RimWorld)). # Terraria UI/UX — Cited Fact Digest

## 1. Inventory & crafting UI

- The player inventory is **40 main slots in four rows of ten, plus a 10-slot hotbar** mapped to the 0–9 keys; additional dedicated slots exist for coins (4), ammo (4), trash (1), armor (3), vanity armor, dyes, accessories (5–6), and equipment (5) ([Inventory — Official Terraria Wiki](https://terraria.wiki.gg/wiki/Inventory)).
- The hotbar is "a single row of ten, each of which corresponds to the [0-9] keys"; one slot is always highlighted when the inventory is closed, and the hotbar can be **locked via toggle** to prevent accidental item swapping ([Inventory — Official Terraria Wiki](https://terraria.wiki.gg/wiki/Inventory)).
- The crafting menu opens automatically with the inventory and is an **available-recipes-only display**, not a full recipe browser: "recipes will not be displayed if the player does not possess all of the ingredients." Items in an open storage container count toward recipe availability, and the menu supports scroll and list modes ([Inventory — Official Terraria Wiki](https://terraria.wiki.gg/wiki/Inventory)).
- **1.2 (September 30, 2013)** added "an extended crafting menu that will show everything you can craft," increased inventory space by 10 slots, doubled chest capacity, and raised some stack sizes to 999 ([1.2 — Official Terraria Wiki](https://terraria.wiki.gg/wiki/1.2)).
- **Deposit All** moves all inventory items (starting bottom-right) into an open storage item (starting top-left), stacking where possible — the inverse of **Loot All**; a **Quick Stack** button appears at the bottom-left when a chest is open ([Storage items — Terraria Wiki](https://terraria.fandom.com/wiki/Storage_items)).
- **1.3.0.1 (June 30, 2015)**: "Added an inventory button to Quick Stack to All Nearby Chests, making inventory management much quicker"; item **favoriting** via Alt+Left Click (favorited items cannot be quick-trashed, quick-stacked, or deposited); new dedicated Minecart, Mount, Pet, and Grappling Hook slots; item linking in chat; info accessories working from inventory without being equipped ([1.3.0.1 — Official Terraria Wiki](https://terraria.wiki.gg/wiki/1.3.0.1)).
- **1.3.1 (May 22, 2016)** added an **Inventory Sorting** button and "smart interact" for chatting with NPCs, opening doors, and using objects ([1.3.1 — Official Terraria Wiki](https://terraria.wiki.gg/wiki/1.3.1)).
- **1.4.0.1 (May 16, 2020)**: the currently selected recipe "now has a gold highlight"; sorting attempts to organize coin slots sequentially; the **Void Bag/Void Vault** added portable overflow storage; players can craft from an open chest without moving its contents ([1.4.0.1 — Official Terraria Wiki](https://terraria.wiki.gg/wiki/1.4.0.1); [Storage items — Terraria Wiki](https://terraria.fandom.com/wiki/Storage_items)).
- **1.4.4 ("Labor of Love", 2022)** raised max stack size from 999 to **9999**, added **Equipment Loadouts** (up to 3 sets, default keys F1–F3, swappable mid-fight), and gave Quick Stack to Nearby Chests a **visual effect** "showing the items which are being quick stacked moving towards the chests they go into" ([1.4.4 — Official Terraria Wiki](https://terraria.wiki.gg/wiki/1.4.4); [Inventory — Official Terraria Wiki](https://terraria.wiki.gg/wiki/Inventory)).
- **1.4.5** reworked the crafting UI with **category tabs and a search bar** and allows crafting from materials stored in nearby chests ([Godlike Host — New Crafting UI Explained in Terraria 1.4.5](https://godlike.host/new-crafting-ui-explained-in-terraria-1-4-5-update-how-to-craft-from-nearby-chests-blog/); the 1.4.5 "Bigger and Boulder" update went live with full patch notes in January 2026 ([Nintendo Life](https://www.nintendolife.com/news/2026/01/terrarias-huge-bigger-and-boulder-update-is-now-live-here-are-the-patch-notes))).

## 2. HUD design

- **Health/mana**: since 1.4, players can switch among **six "Health and Mana Style" options** — Classic (hearts/stars plus numeric health), Fancy, Fancy 2, Bars, Bars 2, and Bars 3 (bar display with numeric readouts) ([Health — Official Terraria Wiki](https://terraria.wiki.gg/wiki/Health); [Terraria Community Forums thread on health/mana styles](https://forums.terraria.org/index.php?threads/improvement-of-health-and-mana-style.90368/)).
- **Buffs** were introduced in **Desktop 1.0.5** (18 initial buffs); "active buffs are shown as icons below the hotbar, along with their remaining duration," and can be right-clicked to cancel. The simultaneous display cap rose from 10 to 22 in 1.2.2 and to **44 combined buffs/debuffs** in 1.4.4 ([Buffs — Official Terraria Wiki](https://terraria.wiki.gg/wiki/Buffs)).
- **Map/minimap** arrived in **1.2**: "a map, mini map, and overlay that only shows tiles the brightest they have ever been seen by the player," toggled with Tab/M or map icons; 1.2 also added **enemy health bars** ([1.2 — Official Terraria Wiki](https://terraria.wiki.gg/wiki/1.2)).
- **1.3.0.1** added **boss minimap icons**, invasion-event **progress bars**, breath/lava-immunity bars above the player's head, faded damage numbers for other players' hits in multiplayer, and a last-death marker on the map ([1.3.0.1 — Official Terraria Wiki](https://terraria.wiki.gg/wiki/1.3.0.1)).
- A dedicated **boss health bar** did not exist until **Desktop 1.4.0.1**: "a red health bar will appear on their screen that represents the boss' total remaining health," shown at the bottom of the screen (below the minimap on mobile), with a togglable "Boss Health Bar Numbers" setting, the boss's map icon on the left, and automatic tracking of the most recently damaged or closest boss. Special cases: Eater of Worlds shows total segment health; Celestial Pillars show a dual shield+health bar ([Boss health bar — Official Terraria Wiki](https://terraria.wiki.gg/wiki/Boss_health_bar)).
- 1.4.0.1 also gave player, NPC, and boss map icons "a white outline on the map to increase visibility" ([1.4.0.1 — Official Terraria Wiki](https://terraria.wiki.gg/wiki/1.4.0.1)).

## 3. The Guide NPC & discoverability

- The Guide spawns near the player when a world is created and is usually the first NPC encountered; via his "Crafting" option he "can tell the player what can be crafted with" any item shown to him, including the required crafting station. This **recipe-lookup feature was added in Desktop 1.0.5** ([Guide — Official Terraria Wiki](https://terraria.wiki.gg/wiki/Guide)).
- The Guide's Help dialogue is **dynamic**, adjusting to bosses defeated, NPCs present, inventory contents, and player stats; **1.4.0.1** "massively overhauled the Guide's help dialogue, adding hints and tips for content all the way up to the Lunar Events, addressing all new NPCs since 1.1, replacing outdated information" ([Guide — Official Terraria Wiki](https://terraria.wiki.gg/wiki/Guide)).
- Documented early-game opacity criticism: PC Gamer's 2011 review listed **the lack of an in-game tutorial and the slow start as drawbacks** ([PC Gamer review, 2011](https://www.pcgamer.com/terraria-review-2011/)). Destructoid (Darren Nakamura, April 2013): "players are dropped into a procedurally generated world with little direction other than an NPC and night closing fast… Without a handy guide, many would be lost"; the console port added "a tutorial, which was sorely needed to mitigate the aimlessness of the early game" ([Destructoid — Review: Terraria](https://www.destructoid.com/reviews/review-terraria/)). PC Gamer's later re-review states the progression systems — "the way you're able to gear up, or unlock its world-altering bosses — are intentionally opaque" ([PC Gamer — Terraria review](https://www.pcgamer.com/terraria-review/)).
- 1.4's discoverability responses: the **Bestiary** (Desktop 1.4.0.1) is "an in-game catalog of Terraria's various entities" whose enemy entries unlock in **four stages by kill count** (name/portrait → stats → item lists → exact drop rates); it has **60 filters**, multiple sort orders, and search with boss aliases; completing 10% of its 546 entries unlocks the Zoologist NPC ([Bestiary — Official Terraria Wiki](https://terraria.wiki.gg/wiki/Bestiary)).
- **Journey Mode** (1.4.0.1) surfaces item knowledge through a **research/duplication UI in a Power Menu on the inventory screen**: players research fixed quantities of an item (e.g., 10 Life Crystals vs. 1 Heart Lantern) to unlock infinite duplication, alongside time/weather/difficulty sliders ([Journey Mode — Official Terraria Wiki](https://terraria.wiki.gg/wiki/Journey_Mode); [Hold To Reset — research and duplication](https://holdtoreset.com/how-to-research-and-duplicate-items-in-terraria-1-4-journey-mode/)). On mobile, "Research & Duplication are located within Inventory… so you can Research, Duplicate, Craft, and Repeat all without leaving your Inventory Screen" ([505 Games — Terraria 1.4 Mobile FAQ](https://505games.com/terraria-1-4-mobile-update-launch-and-faq-guide/)).

## 4. Controller/console/mobile UI adaptations

- David Welch (creative director/product manager on the console port, working with Engine Software) documented the adaptation. The team shipped **two cursor modes**: "Auto Cursor" (Right-Stick direction auto-targets blocks) and "Manual Cursor" (moves like a mouse for precise building) — "I'd hoped that there would be a single solution for aiming… but the more I played, the more I was convinced that we'd need to include both" ([David Welch, Medium — The making of Terraria for consoles, Part 2: The Controls](https://medium.com/@watsonwelch/terraria-on-console-10-years-later-part-2-the-controls-bb84cac14700)).
- For combat, Welch concluded "the only thing that actually mattered was the direction you wanted to fire," adopting **Geometry Wars-style directional aiming**; LB/RB hotbar cycling followed Minecraft: 360 Edition's convention; Zelda-style **D-pad item assignment** let players bind any inventory item; the grappling hook needed access "independent" of the hotbar, so it got a dedicated LT binding ([Welch, Part 2](https://medium.com/@watsonwelch/terraria-on-console-10-years-later-part-2-the-controls-bb84cac14700)).
- Gamepad play uses a **radial hotbar**; settings let players cycle the hotbar with bumpers instead of the radial interface ([Game controls — Official Terraria Wiki](https://terraria.wiki.gg/wiki/Game_controls)).
- For mobile, Welch describes the PC screen as "multiple distinct user interfaces: a grid of inventory slots, a crafting UI, and character equipment slots" plus chest/merchant panels; mobile **split these onto individual tabbed screens** (mirroring console and Minecraft: Pocket Edition), added a **magnifying-glass cursor** because "it's hard to see under your fingers," and used "a persistently available list of recipes, separated into categories" that unlocks recipes as ingredients are collected "to keep the number of recipes manageable" ([David Welch, Medium — The making of Terraria mobile, Part 3: Crafting a new UI](https://medium.com/@watsonwelch/the-making-of-terraria-mobile-part-3-crafting-a-new-ui-4fb84708c767)).

([1.3.1 — Official Terraria Wiki](https://terraria.wiki.gg/wiki/1.3.1)). 1.4.0.1 further "changed how Gamepad LT and RT buttons navigate through the inventory menus" with full looping, and added an "Interface Movement Delay" cursor-speed option ([1.4.0.1 — Official Terraria Wiki](https://terraria.wiki.gg/wiki/1.4.0.1)).
- Destructoid's console review noted usability friction in the port: equipment switching "requires cycling through each with the shoulder buttons," and D-pad mappings "strangely must be re-mapped every time you start playing" ([Destructoid](https://www.destructoid.com/reviews/review-terraria/)).

# Fact digest: UIs of LLM-agent world simulations (Smallville and descendants)

## 1. Stanford Generative Agents / Smallville (Park et al., UIST 2023)

**Rendering and world presentation.** The paper states "The Smallville sandbox game environment is built using the Phaser web game development framework," with visual environment sprites, agent avatars, an environment map, and a collision map imported into Phaser ([ar5iv HTML of arXiv:2304.03442](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2304.03442)). Agents appear as "simple sprite avatar[s]" in an overhead pixel-art view of a small town containing houses, a café, a bar, stores, a park, and a college, with the environment modeled as a tree of areas and objects ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2304.03442)).

**How agent actions are displayed.** Agent behavior is surfaced ambiently via emoji: when an agent performs an action such as "Isabella Rodriguez is writing in her journal," the system "utilizes a language model to translate the action into a set of emojis, which appear above each avatar's head in a speech bubble" ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2304.03442)). The inspector pattern is click-to-expand: "The complete natural language description of the action can be accessed by clicking on the agent's avatar" ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2304.03442)). Third-party documentation of the released system describes the dashboard as showing "the memory stream, current activities, locations, and emojis of all available agents" ([Emergent Mind topic page](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/generative-agents-smallville)).

**Conversations.** Agents "communicate with each other in full natural language," and conversations are rendered as quoted dialogue exchanges (e.g., agents discussing the mayoral election or Isabella's Valentine's Day party) ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2304.03442)).

**User intervention (three documented modes).**
1. *Interview / persona conversation:* "The user communicates with the agent through natural language by specifying a persona that the agent should perceive them as" — e.g. addressing an agent as a "news reporter" and asking questions ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2304.03442)).
2. *Inner voice:* "To directly command one of the agents, the user takes on the persona of the agent's 'inner voice'—this makes the agent more likely to treat the statement as a directive" ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2304.03442)).
3. *Embodiment and environment rewriting:* "Users and agents can influence the state of the objects in this world"; end users "can also reshape an agent's environment in Smallville by rewriting the status of objects surrounding the agent in natural language" (e.g., setting a stove to "burning"), and "the agent that the user embodies can be an agent already present in the world... or it can be an outside visitor" ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2304.03442)).

**Server architecture (as it affects the UI).** "The server maintains a JSON data structure that contains information about each agent... At each sandbox time step, the sandbox server parses the JSON for any changes coming from the generative agents," moves avatars, and updates the state of sandbox objects agents interact with ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2304.03442)). The released code splits into an "environment server" (Django, serving the browser UI at `localhost:8000`) and a "simulation server" (`reverie.py`), run concurrently; the user watches agents move on the map at `http://localhost:8000/simulator_home` and can pan the map with keyboard arrows ([joonspk-research/generative_agents README](https://github.com/joonspk-research/generative_agents)).

**Replay vs. demo modes.** The repo distinguishes a *Replay* mode (`/replay/<sim-name>/<step>`, "intended primarily for debugging," with plain character rendering) from a *Demo* mode (`/demo/<sim-name>/<step>/<speed>`) that shows proper character sprites and takes an adjustable playback speed parameter from 1 (slowest) to 5 (fastest); demos require first compressing a saved simulation with `compress_sim_storage.py` into `compressed_storage` ([README](https://github.com/joonspk-research/generative_agents)). The repo ships pre-computed simulations (e.g., `July1_the_ville_isabella_maria_klaus-step-3-20`) viewable without running any LLM calls ([README](https://github.com/joonspk-research/generative_agents)).

**The public demo site.** The paper links a hosted pre-computed replay: "A demonstration of an actual simulation of the generative agent society can be viewed at the following link: https://reverie.herokuapp.com/UIST_Demo/" ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2304.03442)); an earlier `arXiv_Demo` replay also existed at [reverie.herokuapp.com/arXiv_Demo/](https://reverie.herokuapp.com/arXiv_Demo/), where viewers could click character sprites to see an agent's current state in detail ([demo page](https://reverie.herokuapp.com/arXiv_Demo/)).

**Evaluation-as-UI.** The believability evaluation was itself an interview UI pattern: researchers "interview" agents to "probe their ability to remember past experiences, plan future actions... react appropriately to unexpected events, and reflect on their performance"; human evaluators "watched a replay of a randomly chosen agent's life in Smallville" and "had access to all information stored in the agent's memory stream" ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2304.03442)).

**Stated cost/scale limitation.** "The present study required substantial time and resources to simulate 25 agents for two days, costing thousands of dollars in token credits and taking multiple days to complete" — the explicit reason the public demo is a pre-computed replay rather than a live simulation ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2304.03442)).

## 2. AI Town (a16z-infra)

**What it is.** AI Town is "a MIT-licensed, deployable starter kit for building and customizing your own version of AI town — a virtual town where AI characters live, chat and socialize," inspired by the Stanford paper; it was built by a16z's Yoko Li and Martin Casado with Convex CTO James Cowling ([a16z-infra/ai-town README](https://github.com/a16z-infra/ai-town/blob/main/README.md); [AiCoin coverage](https://www.aicoin.com/en/article/361574)).

**Rendering and UI.** Pixel-art graphics with 16x16 game asset tilesets and animated character spritesheets, all rendered with PixiJS; the architecture doc states "AI Town uses `pixi-react` to render the game state to the browser for human consumption" ([README](https://github.com/a16z-infra/ai-town/blob/main/README.md); [ARCHITECTURE.md](https://github.com/a16z-infra/ai-town/blob/main/ARCHITECTURE.md)). A hosted live demo runs at [convex.dev/ai-town](https://www.convex.dev/ai-town) ([README](https://github.com/a16z-infra/ai-town/blob/main/README.md)).

**Spectate vs. participate.** Users can spectate the running world or join it as a character; human players "may be associated with a human user," and AI Town 2.0 explicitly added "Human interactivity, Code extensibility, Better modding support, Game engine overhaul" ([README](https://github.com/a16z-infra/ai-town/blob/main/README.md); [Convex tweet on AI Town 2.0](https://x.com/convex/status/1719451086908702824)). Human participants join a world, navigate by clicking a destination ("players specify where they want to go, and the engine figures out how to get there" — RTS-style pathfinding), and engage in conversations through invite/accept/reject states with membership tracked as "invited, walking over, or actively participating"; typing indicators (`startTyping` / `finishSendingMessage`) prevent humans and agents talking over each other ([ARCHITECTURE.md](https://github.com/a16z-infra/ai-town/blob/main/ARCHITECTURE.md)).

**Conversation display.** Clicking on a character lets the user view/interact with it; conversations are first-class objects created by a player and ended at a point in time, with the conversation layer exposing `startConversation`, `continueConversation`, and `leaveConversation`, and agent replies injected with personality and memories via prompt engineering ([ARCHITECTURE.md](https://github.com/a16z-infra/ai-town/blob/main/ARCHITECTURE.md); [README](https://github.com/a16z-infra/ai-town/blob/main/README.md)).

**World/time controls.** The UI has a button to "manually freeze & unfreeze the world"; "the simulation will pause after 5 minutes if the window is idle" and can be resumed from the interface ([README](https://github.com/a16z-infra/ai-town/blob/main/README.md)). Under the hood the engine runs 60Hz ticks but persists state once per second ("steps"), giving roughly 1.5 seconds of input latency; for smooth animation "the client receives both the current value _and_ the past step's worth of history, and it can 'replay' the history to make the motion smooth" via `useHistoricalValue` / `useHistoricalTime` hooks — a built-in micro-replay mechanism in the live UI ([ARCHITECTURE.md](https://github.com/a16z-infra/ai-town/blob/main/ARCHITECTURE.md)).

**Extensibility pattern.** To add gameplay elements both humans and agents can use, developers "add the feature to convex/aiTown, render it in the UI in src/, and respond to it in convex/aiTown/agent.ts" ([ARCHITECTURE.md](https://github.com/a16z-infra/ai-town/blob/main/ARCHITECTURE.md)). Backend state, vector search for memories, and transactions live in Convex; Clerk provides optional auth and Replicate/MusicGen background music ([README](https://github.com/a16z-infra/ai-town/blob/main/README.md); [Convex Stack post on character identities](https://stack.convex.dev/building-ai-town-character-ids)).

## 3. Other clones and descendants

**AgentSims (2023).** "AgentSims: An Open-Source Sandbox for Large Language Model Evaluation" (arXiv:2308.04026) pitches task-based evaluation where "LLM agents complete tasks in a simulated environment"; its differentiator is authoring-by-UI: "researchers can build their evaluation tasks by adding agents and buildings on an interactive GUI," with memory/planning/tool-use systems swappable in a few lines of code; a hosted demo was published at agentsims.com and code at [py499372727/AgentSims](https://github.com/py499372727/AgentSims) ([arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.04026)).

**Project Sid (Altera, 2024).** "Project Sid: Many-agent simulations toward AI civilization" (arXiv:2411.00114) ran 10–1,000+ agents in Minecraft under the PIANO architecture, which "enables agents to interact with humans and other agents in real-time while maintaining coherence across multiple output streams" ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00114v1)). Observation of the society was primarily post-hoc analytics rather than a live inspector UI: the paper plots specialization entropy and role distributions over time, taxation-compliance rates, and cultural transmission — using "LM calls to convert agent conversations into memes," a directed social-relationship graph with sentiment-colored edges (Fig. 7A), and geographic religion-spread maps ("the total area of Pastafarian influence... increased with time," Fig. 12D) ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00114v1)). A documented scale limitation: simulations over 1,000 agents "exceeded the computational constraints of our Minecraft server environment, causing agents to be sporadically unresponsive" ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00114v1)). Because the world is a stock Minecraft server, agents were watchable through the ordinary Minecraft client/spectator view, and publicized behaviors (merchant hub, constitution amended via Google Docs voting, Pastafarianism spread through bribery) were reported from those observations ([Kaggle discussion](https://www.kaggle.com/general/531987); [Tom's Guide coverage](https://www.tomsguide.com/ai/a-company-gave-1000-ai-agents-access-to-minecraft-and-they-built-a-society); [altera-al/project-sid repo](https://github.com/altera-al/project-sid/blob/main/README.md)).

**Community re-implementations of Smallville.** Multiple forks/ports of the Stanford repo exist with the same Django+Phaser replay UI (e.g., [semantic-systems/generative_agents_](https://github.com/semantic-systems/generative_agents_/), [TrustAlgo/Interactive-Simulacra-of-Human-Behavior](https://github.com/TrustAlgo/Interactive-Simulacra-of-Human-Behavior), [Vaishya26/Stanford-Town-AI-Project](https://github.com/Vaishya26/Stanford-Town-AI-Project)); a separate project, [nmatter1/smallville](https://github.com/nmatter1/smallville), packages "Generative Agents for video games" based on the paper. The open-sourcing (July 2023) was widely covered as enabling anyone to run and view the town locally ([Medium: "Stanford Smallville is officially open-source!"](https://rikiphukon.medium.com/stanford-smallville-is-officially-open-source-9882e3fbc981)); the prompt internals visible in the repo were dissected on Hacker News ([HN thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=37088105)).

**Commercial LLM-NPC demos (first-person, voice-driven UIs — a different pattern).**
- *NVIDIA ACE "Kairos" (with Convai, Computex 2023):* a real-time Unreal Engine 5 demo where the player character Kai walks into "Jin's Ramen shop" and converses with the NPC Jin by speaking into a microphone; NVIDIA Riva handles speech recognition and text-to-speech, Omniverse Audio2Face drives facial animation — i.e., the "UI" is diegetic voice conversation, not an inspector panel ([NVIDIA GeForce news](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/geforce/news/nvidia-ace-for-games-generative-ai-npcs/); [Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/news/nvidia-ace-brings-npcs-to-life); [PC Gamer hands-on](https://www.pcgamer.com/i-spoke-to-an-nvidia-ai-powered-npc-about-his-ramen-and-his-responses-were-frighteningly-good/)). NVIDIA later added NeMo SteerLM sliders for adjusting NPC personality/emotion attributes ([NVIDIA ACE SteerLM post](https://www.nvidia.com/en-gb/geforce/news/nvidia-ace-steerlm-npc-personalities)).
- *Inworld "Origins" (Steam demo, 2023):* an Unreal Engine 5 detective mystery set in 2030 where the player interrogates AI NPCs through open-ended voice-based interaction to solve a bombing case; shipped as a free Steam demo during Steam Next Fest ([Inworld tweet](https://x.com/inworld_ai/status/1671171197281931266); [Steam page](https://steamcommunity.com/app/2199920); [New Atlas coverage](https://newatlas.com/games/inworld-origins-ai-npc)).

## 4. Cross-cutting UX observations documented in sources

- **Replay, not live, as the default public experience:** Stanford's public demo was explicitly a "pre-computed replay of a simulation... for demonstration purposes" because live simulation cost "thousands of dollars in token credits" and "multiple days" of wall-clock time for two game days ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2304.03442); [README](https://github.com/joonspk-research/generative_agents)). AI Town inverts this — a single continuously-live hosted world — but throttles cost with idle-pausing after 5 minutes and a manual freeze button ([README](https://github.com/a16z-infra/ai-town/blob/main/README.md)).
- **Time/speed controls:** Smallville's demo mode exposes playback speed 1–5 and an arbitrary starting step in the URL ([README](https://github.com/joonspk-research/generative_agents)); AI Town's engine replays one second of positional history client-side for smoothness and pauses/unpauses the whole world ([ARCHITECTURE.md](https://github.com/a16z-infra/ai-town/blob/main/ARCHITECTURE.md)).
- **Progressive disclosure of agent internals:** the dominant inspector pattern is ambient glyph (emoji speech bubble) → click avatar → full natural-language action, plus memory-stream access; evaluators auditing believability were given "all information stored in the agent's memory stream" while watching a single agent's replay — i.e., following one agent at a time, not all 25 at once ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2304.03442); [Emergent Mind](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/generative-agents-smallville)).
- **At 500–1000+ agents, per-agent inspection gives way to aggregate dashboards:** Project Sid surfaced societies via metrics (entropy, sentiment graphs, spatial spread of religion) computed with LM post-processing of conversations, and hit hard server limits past 1,000 agents ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00114v1)).

# Fact Digest: UI/UX of Conversational AI Interfaces — Chat Turns and Agentic-Loop Rendering

## 1. Chat-turn rendering conventions (ChatGPT-style web UIs)

**Streaming token-by-token output.** ChatGPT renders each generated token in real time as it flows from the model to the frontend; one system-design analysis describes the frontend as "not just a chat box — it's a high-performance streaming client optimized for perceived latency," with "every layer designed around streaming — from the browser consuming SSE chunks to the GPU generating tokens autoregressively" ([ChatGPT system design analysis](https://atharvanaik.me/posts/chatgpt-system-design-architecture)). A developer writeup on token streaming reports first-token latency dropping "by almost 90%" when switching from full-response delivery to streaming, and observes that "when people see something happening, they wait. When it's just a spinner, they bail" ([Streaming Tokens, Triple Speed — Medium](https://medium.com/@connect.hashblock/streaming-tokens-triple-speed-4704da5afb4d)). Latency benchmarking articles treat time-to-first-token (TTFT) and inter-token latency as the two governing metrics for streaming chat UIs ([5 LLM APIs tested for latency](https://dev.to/kunal_d6a8fea2309e1571ee7/5-llm-apis-tested-for-latency-real-data-2026-3e4o)); OpenAI's own latency-optimization guide frames streaming as a first-class latency technique ([OpenAI latency optimization guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/latency-optimization)). A design breakdown of ChatGPT's interface argues streaming "is not a performance optimization. It is a trust mechanism," serving three functions: reducing perceived latency, signaling active processing, and sustaining engagement during long responses ([ChatGPT Design Breakdown — 925 Studios](https://www.925studios.co/blog/chatgpt-interface-design-breakdown)).

**Streaming has documented downsides.** NN/g research found streaming long responses can be "overwhelming and disorienting": Mississippi's government chatbot MISSI streamed long answers while autoscrolling users to the end, "making it impossible for users to read anything until it had stopped"; one participant said "the pouring in of information kind of made me feel overwhelmed" ([NN/g: Less Chat, More Answer](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/less-chat-more-answer/)). The same study documents demand for scannable formatting — a participant praising a bulleted, bold-headed response ("Oh, I love this (...) it's not just like one big paragraph") and NN/g recommending direct, scannable, expandable responses. An academic line of work ("Streaming, Fast and Slow") studies cognitive-load-aware pacing of streamed LLM output as a serving-system concern ([arXiv 2504.17999](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.17999)).

**Document-style turns, not bubbles.** The 925 Studios breakdown states ChatGPT uses a document-style format rather than chat bubbles, with a fixed-width response column constrained to "roughly 65 characters per line, which is the optimal reading line length for dense text," while "most AI products ignore this and let responses span the full viewport width" ([925 Studios](https://www.925studios.co/blog/chatgpt-interface-design-breakdown)). The same source documents the blank-field-first empty state ("a blank text field at the center of the screen. No instructions. No templates.") and the recency-grouped conversation sidebar ("Today, Yesterday, Last 7 Days") functioning as "external memory," while noting the absence of within-conversation content search. Its list of ChatGPT's most-copied patterns: blank-field empty state, streaming text output, outcome-labeled model selectors, conversation timeline navigation, fixed-width response column.

**Edit / regenerate / stop affordances.** ChatGPT's UI includes an edit affordance on user messages and a regenerate affordance on assistant messages, with a version selector ("0/2"-style counters and arrows) for navigating regenerated/edited branches — documented extensively (mostly via breakage reports) on OpenAI's forum ([response-selector/edit-button bug thread](https://community.openai.com/t/chatgpt-bug-introduced-in-response-selector-of-regenerations-and-edits-edit-button-goes-missing/1145664), [regenerate button reports](https://community.openai.com/t/the-regenerate-button-has-disappeared/70110)). The stop-generation button historically let users "stop a response and immediately refine their message"; a later change made clicking Stop produce an error state requiring regeneration before editing, prompting a restore request ([Restore ChatGPT's Stop Button — OpenAI forum](https://community.openai.com/t/restore-chatgpts-stop-button-without-errors/1132068)).

**NN/g's broader LLM-interface research.** Analyzing 425 interactions with ChatGPT, Bard, and Bing Chat, NN/g identified six distinct conversation types (from vague underspecified prompts to razor-sharp questions), concluding different conversation types "require varied interfaces" ([NN/g: 6 Types of Conversations with Generative AI](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/AI-conversation-types/)). NN/g also documents "prompt controls" — UI components surrounding the input field that expedite/supplement text input, creating "hybrid UIs" beyond the bare prompt box ([NN/g: Prompt Controls in GenAI Chatbots](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/prompt-controls-genai/)), plus "response outlining" behavior with generative chatbots ([NN/g: Response Outlining](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/response-outlining/)) and a diary study of information foraging across three chatbots ([NN/g: Information Foraging with Generative AI](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/generative-ai-diary/)).

## 2. Claude Code's terminal UI

**Stack.** Claude Code's UI is written in TypeScript/React using the Ink framework for interactive CLI elements, with Meta's open-source Yoga as the layout engine, bundled with Bun; per the Pragmatic Engineer's interview-based writeup, "terminal-based applications have the disadvantage of needing to support all sizes of terminals, so you need a layout system to do this pragmatically" ([How Claude Code is built — Pragmatic Engineer](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/how-claude-code-is-built)). The same piece documents the team's prototyping velocity — ~21 todo-list UI prototype iterations in 2 days — and Boris Cherny's minimalism ("Every time there's a new model release, we delete a bunch of code"). Ink's own README lists Claude Code among apps built with it, alongside GitHub Copilot CLI, Wrangler, Gatsby, Terraform CDK, and Prisma, and describes itself as "React for CLIs" with Yoga-backed Flexbox ("CSS-like properties"), `<Box>` as "display: flex" for terminals, `<Text>` for styled text, and a `<Static>` component for output that should be written once and remain permanently above the live/scrollable region ([Ink on GitHub](https://github.com/vadimdemedes/ink)).

**Custom renderer beyond stock Ink.** A dev.to analysis of Claude Code's source reports that "React handles reconciliation. A custom renderer handles layout, screen buffers, diffing, and high-FPS terminal updates" — a pipeline of React commit → Yoga layout → screen buffer → damage-aware diffing → terminal patches "flushed in a single write." It documents packed typed arrays (`Int32Array`, `BigInt64Array`) with intern pools instead of object-per-cell buffers, keyboard input mapped to React "discrete" priority while resize/scroll are "continuous," and clean-subtree cell copying instead of re-rendering ([How Claude Code Uses React in the Terminal](https://dev.to/vilvaathibanpb/how-claude-code-uses-react-in-the-terminal-2f3b)). A developer who studied the source describes the fork as "beyond recognition": double-buffered rendering with cell-level diffing and an optimizer merging adjacent writes into minimal escape sequences, running "at 60fps on a 200-column terminal while streaming tokens" ([I studied Claude Code's leaked source — dev.to](https://dev.to/minnzen/i-studied-claude-codes-leaked-source-and-built-a-terminal-ui-toolkit-from-it-4poh)). Additional architectural walkthroughs: [Claude Code Internals Part 11 — Terminal UI](https://kotrotsos.medium.com/claude-code-internals-part-11-terminal-ui-542fe17db016) (confirms "components render based on state… instead of DOM elements, Ink produces terminal output with colors, borders, and layout") and [DeepWiki's UI Layer page](https://deepwiki.com/farion1231/claude-code/10-ui-layer-(inkreact-terminal)).

**Documented interactive-mode behaviors** (from the official docs, [code.claude.com/docs/en/interactive-mode](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/interactive-mode)):
- **Collapsed tool use + transcript viewer:** `Ctrl+O` toggles a transcript viewer that "shows detailed tool usage and execution, with a timestamp and the model used on each assistant message. Also expands MCP calls, which collapse to a single line like 'Called slack 3 times' by default." I.e., tool-use detail is collapsed by default and expandable on demand.
- **Interrupt:** `Esc` "interrupt[s] Claude… Stop the current response or tool call mid-turn so you can redirect. Claude keeps the work done so far"; when a dialog such as a permission prompt is open, Esc closes the dialog instead. Double-`Esc` on an empty prompt opens a rewind menu (checkpointing) "to restore or summarize code and conversation from a previous point."
- **Permission modes / plan mode:** `Shift+Tab` cycles permission modes — `default` (labeled "Manual" in the mode indicator), `acceptEdits`, `plan`, plus opt-in modes like `auto` or `bypassPermissions`. Plan mode holds back all writes/commands for the session while allowing read/search/explore ([permission-modes docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/permission-modes); [third-party plan-mode guide](https://claudecode101.com/en/mechanics/plan-mode)).
- **Todo checklist:** `Ctrl+T` toggles "Claude's to-do checklist" in the status area — "items Claude created to plan multi-step work, with indicators showing what's pending, in progress, or complete," displaying up to five tasks at a time; it is explicitly separate from the background-task view (`/tasks` shows running shells and subagents).
- The docs also describe a fullscreen rendering mode with a `?` shortcut-reference panel inside the transcript viewer, and `/tui` to check which renderer is active.

**Permission prompts and approval fatigue.** By default Claude Code requests user approval before executing commands or modifying files. Anthropic's engineering blog reports "Claude Code users approve 93% of permission prompts," producing fatigue where "people stop paying close attention to what they're approving"; auto mode "delegates approvals to model-based classifiers — a middle ground between manual review and no guardrails," achieving a 0.4% false-positive rate on real traffic while missing 17% of genuinely dangerous actions ([Anthropic: How we built Claude Code auto mode](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/claude-code-auto-mode)).

**Status/context visibility.** Claude Code exposes a customizable status line whose input includes a `context_window` object with `used_percentage` and `remaining_percentage` fields from the most recent API response ([statusline docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/statusline)); the interface also surfaces a "Context left until auto-compact: N%" indicator as the window fills ([explainer on the auto-compact indicator](https://lalatenduswain.medium.com/understanding-context-left-until-auto-compact-0-in-claude-cli-b7f6e43a62dc)), and community status-line configs render a color-coded progress bar of model, tokens remaining, and autocompact threshold ([example gist](https://gist.github.com/plribeiro3000/17354a5214a97f59c8fef9e37b30c87e)). **Subagents** are documented as isolated Claude instances with their own context windows, tools, and permissions that "go off, do one focused thing, and report back a summary" to the main transcript ([Claude Code agents explained](https://joseparreogarcia.substack.com/p/claude-code-agents-explained)).

## 3. Agentic-loop rendering patterns generally

**Human-in-the-loop approval UIs.** LangChain's HITL documentation describes the canonical pattern: "let your agent pause execution, present the pending action to the user, and resume only after explicit approval," built on LangGraph interrupts and checkpoints so "the pause is durable"; `interrupt()` accepts any JSON-serializable value, enabling the backend to hand the frontend a renderable "card" (form type, title, context under review, fields to collect) ([LangChain HITL docs](https://docs.langchain.com/oss/python/langchain/frontend/human-in-the-loop)). LangChain's runtime-design post states that interrupt/resume "without having to redo work done until then enables many essential UX patterns for AI agents… approve or reject actions, edit the next action, ask clarifying questions, or even time travel to re-do things from a previous step" ([Building LangGraph](https://www.langchain.com/blog/building-langgraph)). LangGraph also documents "first-class streaming of intermediate events" as a production requirement distinct from token streaming ([LangGraph patterns overview](https://callsphere.ai/blog/langgraph-multi-agent-workflow-deep-dive)).

**Collapsible reasoning / "thinking" affordances.** Open WebUI documents the now-standard pattern for reasoning models (DeepSeek R1, o1): reasoning content wrapped in think-tags is extracted and "rendered in a collapsible UI element labeled 'Thought' or 'Thinking'" to keep the chat clean while preserving the logic ([Open WebUI reasoning-model docs](https://docs.openwebui.com/features/chat-conversations/chat-features/reasoning-models/)). For OpenAI o1, ChatGPT shows a *summarized* chain of thought (e.g., "I noticed a mismatch between the first letters of 1 Across and 1 Down") while raw reasoning tokens are hidden — and still billed; OpenAI's stated rationale is safety ("reason about how it's obeying those policy rules without exposing intermediary steps") and competitive protection against distillation; Simon Willison called hiding it "a big step backwards" for interpretability ([Simon Willison on o1](https://simonwillison.net/2024/Sep/12/openai-o1/); [OpenAI: Learning to reason with LLMs](https://openai.com/index/learning-to-reason-with-llms/)).

**Streaming as the default agent-UI substrate.** Vercel AI SDK documentation and guides state that streaming "deliver[s] tokens to the client as they are generated, giving users immediate feedback and dramatically reducing perceived latency," with waiting for the entire output called "an unacceptable user experience" ([AI SDK streaming guide](https://ai-sdk.guide/streaming/)); the SDK supports streaming arbitrary custom data alongside tokens for richer turn rendering ([AI SDK UI: Streaming Custom Data](https://ai-sdk.dev/docs/ai-sdk-ui/streaming-data)). A documented generative-UI latency gap: when an LLM triggers a tool, the client must wait for the full JSON argument payload before rendering the component — "the user stares at a spinner for 3-5 seconds, and then the chart violently pops into the DOM" — motivating a "speculative streaming" proposal that renders a skeleton the moment the tool *name* resolves ([vercel/ai issue #13469](https://github.com/vercel/ai/issues/13469)).

**Interruptibility as convention.** Claude Code documents Esc-to-interrupt with work preserved (above); ChatGPT's Stop button is the web equivalent (above); LangGraph interrupts are the framework-level equivalent. Guides on Claude Code's autonomous modes note that "even in YOLO mode, you can interrupt execution at any time" ([autonomous-mode guide](https://pasqualepillitteri.it/en/news/141/claude-code-dangerously-skip-permissions-guide-autonomous-mode)).

## 4. Terminal-UI rendering tech and constraints

**Ink (JS/React).** Documented capabilities: React component model with hooks (`useInput` for keyboard, `useStdin`/`useStdout`, window-size responsiveness), Yoga Flexbox layout, `<Static>` for permanent write-once output above the live region — the mechanism by which a chat transcript can accumulate in native terminal scrollback while only the bottom "live" area (spinner, prompt, status) rerenders ([Ink README](https://github.com/vadimdemedes/ink)). The process "stay[s] alive only while there is active work in the event loop." Claude Code's need to fork Ink's renderer for 60fps streaming (packed cell buffers, cell-level diffing, merged escape-sequence writes) is documented in the source-analysis writeups cited in §2.

**Bubble Tea (Go).** Official description: a framework "based on The Elm Architecture… well-suited for simple and complex terminal applications, either inline, full-window, or a mix of both," featuring "a high-performance cell-based renderer, built-in color downsampling, declarative views, high-fidelity keyboard and mouse handling, native clipboard support" ([charmbracelet/bubbletea](https://github.com/charmbracelet/bubbletea); [pkg.go.dev](https://pkg.go.dev/github.com/charmbracelet/bubbletea)). The Elm MVU structure — Model (state), Update (message → state), View (state → frame) — maps agent events onto UI re-renders; a chat-client writeup documents network I/O in background goroutines sending messages that trigger re-renders while the user types/scrolls/resizes ([Bubble Tea IRC client deep dive](https://sngeth.com/go/terminal/ui/bubble-tea/2025/08/17/building-terminal-ui-with-bubble-tea/)).

**Textual/Rich (Python).** Textual, by the Rich team, is a "Rapid Application Development framework for Python" with 40+ widgets (DataTable, TextArea, TabbedContent, Markdown, etc.), a web-CSS-like "Textual CSS" styling system, and apps that "run in the terminal or a web browser" and over SSH ([textual.textualize.io](https://textual.textualize.io/)). Its showcased applications include Toad, described as an AI coding interface — i.e., a Textual-based agent console in the Claude Code genre.

**Shared constraint set.** The Pragmatic Engineer piece names the core terminal constraint directly: supporting "all sizes of terminals" requires a real layout system (hence Yoga) ([Pragmatic Engineer](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/how-claude-code-is-built)); the Ink `<Static>` pattern and Bubble Tea's inline-vs-full-window split are the two documented strategies for reconciling live-updating regions with native scrollback.

## 5. Documented UX research and critique

- **Turn/response scannability:** NN/g's site-chatbot study documents that dense unformatted responses filling the viewport discourage engagement, and recommends a "truncated pyramid" (essential answer first, follow-up prompts for detail) ([Less Chat, More Answer](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/less-chat-more-answer/)); its 10 design guidelines for site AI chatbots cover response directness and expandability ([NN/g guidelines](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ai-chatbots-design-guidelines/)).
- **Conversation-type diversity:** the 425-interaction NN/g analysis concluding six conversation types need varied interfaces ([AI Conversation Types](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/AI-conversation-types/)); the older NN/g chatbot usability work (8 participants, mobile+desktop) found chatbots "guide users through simple linear flows and have difficulty when users deviate" ([Experientia summary of NN/g chatbot UX](https://blog.experientia.com/nielsen-norman-group-on-the-user-experience-of-chatbots/)).
- **Trust/transparency:** design-principle syntheses list "handoff willingness, flexibility, proactivity, emotional responsiveness, and transparency" as trust drivers for AI chatbots ([Parallel: UX principles for AI chatbots](https://www.parallelhq.com/blog/ux-ai-chatbots)); the o1 hidden-CoT critique (§3) and the 93%-approval permission-fatigue statistic (§2) are the two most concrete documented data points on transparency of agent actions.
- **Context-window visibility:** surfaced to users as a first-class UI element only in agent consoles (Claude Code's status line percentages and auto-compact countdown, §2); ChatGPT does not expose token usage in its consumer UI (absence noted across the design analyses above).
- **History navigation:** ChatGPT's recency-grouped sidebar with no in-conversation content search; products allowing naming/tagging/project organization "consistently report higher long-term retention than those that default to time-based groupings" ([925 Studios](https://www.925studios.co/blog/chatgpt-interface-design-breakdown)); a practitioner analysis of ChatGPT's conversation-flow UX exists at [hugowph.com](https://www.hugowph.com/blog/the-ux-of-chatgpts-conversation-flow).

# Fact Digest: TUI & Roguelike Interface Design Principles

## 1. Cogmind / Grid Sage Games (Josh Ge, "Kyzrati")

**ASCII as an interface ideal.** In ["Cogmind the Roguelike"](https://www.gridsagegames.com/blog/2015/04/cogmind-roguelike/), Josh Ge argues that ASCII embodies the ideal roguelike interface: "a simple easily readable representation designed to facilitate decision-making." Cogmind ships ASCII as the default with tiles as an accessibility alternative, and every item in the game receives custom ASCII art ([Cogmind the Roguelike](https://www.gridsagegames.com/blog/2015/04/cogmind-roguelike/)).

**Input parity as a hard rule.** Ge's stated rule for Cogmind is that "every action and command must be accessible via both mouse and keyboard," with four different movement command schemes supported simultaneously, and "alphanumeric keyboard commands are embedded directly into the UI wherever they make sense" so hotkeys are discoverable without memorization ([Cogmind the Roguelike](https://www.gridsagegames.com/blog/2015/04/cogmind-roguelike/)).

**Redundant multi-channel feedback.** Cogmind delivers important information through multiple simultaneous channels — colored words, symbols, sound effects, and animation. Example documented by Ge: low health triggers an audio warning, red "ALERT" text, and oscillating interface frames at once ([Cogmind the Roguelike](https://www.gridsagegames.com/blog/2015/04/cogmind-roguelike/)). Cogmind also ships a closed-caption-like audio log and "visible SFX" for hearing-impaired players ([Audio Accessibility Features for Roguelikes, ModDB](https://www.moddb.com/games/cogmind/news/audio-accessibility-features-for-roguelikes)).

**Mixed font dimensions.** Rather than forcing square cells everywhere, Cogmind uses narrow fonts for text-heavy areas and wider (square) rendering for the map — Ge calls this "the best of both worlds" for text readability plus spatial clarity ([Cogmind the Roguelike](https://www.gridsagegames.com/blog/2015/04/cogmind-roguelike/); see also [Fonts in Roguelikes](https://www.gridsagegames.com/blog/2014/09/fonts-in-roguelikes/) and [Readable Text Fonts for Roguelikes](https://www.gridsagegames.com/blog/2015/07/readable-text-fonts-roguelikes/)).

**Map-first information design.** Ge's design goal is to shift "as much information as possible from the message log to the map itself" via "map dynamics" — animated overlays and labels drawn directly on the map — so the player's attention stays on the map rather than the log ([Map Dynamics](https://www.gridsagegames.com/blog/2014/02/map-dynamics/), [Message Log](https://www.gridsagegames.com/blog/2014/02/message-log/)).

**The grid as architecture.** Cogmind is built on a strict 160×60 terminal cell grid — "nearly five times as much display area" as the classic 80×25 terminal — with map cells rendered square by occupying two terminal cells each, and a fixed minimum 50×50-cell map view that gameplay is balanced against ("almost no weapons or active intel should have a range that can exceed the area a player can see by default"). "Upscaling" in this grid-locked architecture means proportionally increasing font/cell size (e.g., reducing rows from 60 to 45 to allow a 33% larger font), not enlarging the grid ([Full UI Upscaling, Part 1: History and Theory](https://www.gridsagegames.com/blog/2024/01/full-ui-upscaling-part-1-history-and-theory/)).

**Accessibility guidance for developers.** In his Roguelike Celebration 2018 talk/post ["How to Make a Roguelike"](https://www.gridsagegames.com/blog/2018/10/how-to-make-a-roguelike/), Ge advises that "accessibility is important" — sufficient documentation, a tutorial, full mouse support, a tileset — while noting some accessibility features must be designed in from the beginning; he also documents that ASCII "makes it *so* easy to add new content" and that his REXPaint ASCII editor is used for "interface design, mapping, and art." Player-metrics from Cogmind show strong adoption of mouse support and tiles alongside ASCII ([How to Make a Roguelike](https://www.gridsagegames.com/blog/2018/10/how-to-make-a-roguelike/)).

## 2. Classic Roguelike UI Conventions (NetHack / DCSS)

**NetHack messaging.** In NetHack's standard ASCII interface, messages display one line at a time at the top of the screen; when more than one line must be shown, NetHack displays a `--More--` prompt and waits for a keypress before showing the rest — explicitly "to give the player time to read" before the screen changes. `Ctrl+P` redisplays previous messages in a format controlled by the `msg_window` option ([NetHack Wiki: Message](https://nethackwiki.com/wiki/Message), [NetHack Wiki: User interface](https://nethackwiki.com/wiki/User_interface)).

**Per-message filtering (NetHack 3.6.0+).** The `MSGTYPE` config option lets users change how individual messages behave: force a `--More--` on specific important messages, suppress repeated printing of a message, or suppress unimportant messages entirely ([NetHack Wiki: MSGTYPE](https://nethackwiki.com/wiki/MSGTYPE)).

**DCSS's explicit interface philosophy.** DCSS's manual contains a formal philosophy section — one of very few roguelikes with one ([Hacker News discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=13127286)). It states: "The interface is radically designed to make gameplay easy - this sounds trivial, but we mean it," and "all tedious, but necessary, chores should be automated." Major goals are "meaningful decisions (no no-brainers)" and "avoidance of grinding," with "painless interface and newbie support" listed among design goals. The manual notes auto-explore "will usually save real time" even when manual play would save in-game turns, and documents Autofight (Tab) and Autofire (Shift-Tab) as combat automation ([crawl_manual.rst, crawl/crawl repo](https://github.com/crawl/crawl/blob/master/crawl-ref/docs/crawl_manual.rst); mirrored at [crawl.akrasiac.org](https://crawl.akrasiac.org/docs/crawl_manual.txt)). The historical philosophy doc is preserved on the [DCSS dev wiki](https://crawl.develz.org/wiki/doku.php?id=graveyard:philosophy).

**Why DCSS's QoL is praised.** Jorge Zhang's essay on DCSS credits autoexplore with removing "the tedium of wandering around the dungeon in favor of quickly getting the player to the interesting decisions that are the meat of the game" ([DCSS: Best Roguelike?](https://www.jorgezhang.com/2020/06/dungeon-crawl-stone-soup-the-greatest-roguelike-of-all-time-and-what-it-can-tell-us-about-game-design/index.html)); HN threads on DCSS's development describe its explicit anti-tedium manifesto as unusual among roguelikes ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=27091885)).

**Targeting systems.** RogueBasin documents that NetHack historically offered direction-only input for ranged actions, while Crawl and Cataclysm implement true targeting systems allowing selection of specific targets for spells and missiles ([RogueBasin: User interface features](https://www.roguebasin.com/index.php/User_interface_features), [mirror](https://chizaruu.github.io/roguebasin/user_interface_features)).

## 3. The Message Log / Event Feed Problem

**Log as secondary channel (Cogmind).** Ge's [Message Log](https://www.gridsagegames.com/blog/2014/02/message-log/) post documents his scheme: the log shows only six lines by default, deliberately small so it stays "a secondary source of information while the player focuses attention on the map"; an "extended log" shows six older lines above (12 total); and the log can expand to full window height (56+ lines) via F4 or click, with animated transitions. He calls the message log "arguably the most vital part of a traditional roguelike's interface" and an "after-the-fact record of why you find yourself in danger."

**Brevity and importance ordering.** Cogmind messages are "very short, like 4-5 words," with the important information placed first in each message; routine movement is never logged, and content is limited to highest-importance combat events (part/robot destruction), status changes, and nearby-unit activity ([Message Log](https://www.gridsagegames.com/blog/2014/02/message-log/)).

**Externalized color schemes.** Cogmind's message colors are controlled from an external text file so modified schemes are possible — documented as an aid for colorblind players ([Message Log](https://www.gridsagegames.com/blog/2014/02/message-log/)).

**Message coalescing (DCSS).** The DCSS dev wiki's interface brainstorm documents message condensation/merging, e.g., collapsing "You hit the imp! You freeze the imp! The imp dies!" into "You freeze the imp to death!" ([DCSS wiki: User Interface Implementable List](https://crawl.develz.org/wiki/doku.php?id=dcss:brainstorm:interface:interface_implementables)). NetHack's equivalent lever is MSGTYPE's suppress-repeats ("norep") and hide options ([NetHack Wiki: MSGTYPE](https://nethackwiki.com/wiki/MSGTYPE)).

**Player-authored log annotations.** Cogmind later added map comments and log notes — players annotating the map and log themselves — documented in [Map Comments and Log Notes](https://www.gridsagegames.com/blog/2021/11/map-comments-and-log-notes/).

## 4. TUI Design Generally

**Terminal cell geometry.** Textual's docs codify a core grid fact: terminal cells are roughly twice as tall as they are wide, so symmetric-looking spacing requires horizontal gutters double the vertical (their documented convention: `grid-gutter: 1 2`) ([Textual: Grid styles](https://textual.textualize.io/styles/grid/)). This is the same asymmetry Cogmind solves by giving map cells two terminal columns ([Full UI Upscaling Part 1](https://www.gridsagegames.com/blog/2024/01/full-ui-upscaling-part-1-history-and-theory/)).

**Color constraints.** Textual documents that terminals expose 16 user-themeable ANSI colors (which apps may override with their own palettes), atop which 256-color and truecolor modes exist ([Textual: Styles guide](https://textual.textualize.io/guide/styles/)). Their layout-design how-to documents a method for grid UIs: sketch first, then "work outside in… starting with the entire space of the terminal," adding outermost content (headers/footers) before inner panes ([Textual: Design a Layout](https://textual.textualize.io/how-to/design-a-layout/)).

**TUI accessibility is worse than assumed.** A widely-circulated 2025/2026 essay ("The text mode lie") argues that "there is a persistent misconception among sighted developers that applications running in a terminal are inherently accessible," and that modern reactive TUI frameworks — Ink (JS/React), Bubble Tea (Go), tcell are named — are "actively destroying the experience for blind users." Documented failure mechanics: the cursor "teleporting all over the screen to update status indicators, spinners, and history" causes screen readers to announce fragments; full-screen re-renders on every keystroke cause multi-second input lag and can destabilize NVDA; and there is no accessibility tree or ARIA equivalent for terminals. Documented mitigations from apps that work well: hiding the cursor entirely (nano, vim), using VT100 hardware scrolling regions instead of rewriting the whole screen (irssi), and keeping the active area a single vertical list (menuconfig). The essay's summary claim: "for the blind user, a dumb, linear CLI stream is infinitely superior to a 'smart' TUI" ([The Inclusive Lens / xogium.me](https://xogium.me/the-text-mode-lie-why-modern-tuis-are-a-nightmare-for-accessibility), covered at [OSnews](https://www.osnews.com/story/144892/the-text-mode-lie-why-modern-tuis-are-a-nightmare-for-accessibility/)).

**WCAG on terminal symbols.** W3C Technique H86 requires ASCII art, emoticons, and non-textual symbols to carry text alternatives for WCAG conformance — applied to CLI contexts in accessibility guidance ([AFixt: Accessible by Design — CLIs](https://afixt.com/accessible-by-design-improving-command-line-interfaces-for-all-users/)). Console-mode screen readers (e.g., yasr; Linux console tooling) are documented in the [FreeBSD accessibility handbook](https://docs.freebsd.org/en/books/accessibility/blindness/) and the [Linux Accessibility HOWTO](https://tldp.org/HOWTO/Accessibility-HOWTO/visual.html). Roguelike-specific design for visually impaired players was the subject of [Roguelike Radio Episode 48](http://www.roguelikeradio.com/2012/10/episode-48-designing-for-visually.html).

## 5. ASCII/Unicode as a Rendering Medium

**Glyph semantics are conventions.** RogueBasin documents the shared symbol vocabulary: `.` for floor, `<`/`>` for stairs, `@` for the protagonist, lowercase letters for monsters, `?` for scrolls, `!` for potions ([RogueBasin: User interface features](https://www.roguebasin.com/index.php/User_interface_features)). Rogue itself (Toy & Wichman, early 1980s) established `@` as the player and relied entirely on character-set graphics — recounted in co-creator Glenn Wichman's history ["Have @ You, You Deadly Zs"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-history-of-rogue-have-you-you-deadly-zs).

**Why ASCII reads fast.** Ge's [ASCII vs. Tiles](https://www.gridsagegames.com/blog/2015/02/ascii-vs-tiles/) documents that players leverage "years of experience distinguishing a very specific set of symbols" so glyphs stay identifiable "even when densely clustered and/or at small sizes"; in his side-by-side comparison "the ASCII here is far clearer despite showing 50% more creatures, and doing so with less space overall." Glyphs "distill information for tactical decision-making into its purest form" with no "superfluous visual distractions." The same post documents ASCII's biggest drawback — players must re-associate learned glyph meanings ("Many players have difficulty making that conceptual leap") — and the market reality that "the average player will always choose to play with a tileset."

**CP437 heritage.** Ge documents that "one of the more common choices for ASCII layout is US code page 437, the standard extended ASCII layout used by games like Dwarf Fortress" — CP437 supplying the box-drawing and shading glyphs used for UI chrome ([Fonts in Roguelikes](https://www.gridsagegames.com/blog/2014/09/fonts-in-roguelikes/)).

## 6. Community-Documented Conventions (RogueBasin / r/roguelikedev)

**Key control conventions.** RogueBasin's "Preferred Key Controls" survey documents the two canonical movement schemes — vi-keys (`y k u / h . l / b j n`; no numpad needed, traditional since Rogue, but has a learning curve and breaks on non-QWERTY layouts) and numpad (`7 8 9 / 4 5 6 / 1 2 3`, "intuitive" but problematic on laptops without numpads) — plus the practice of supporting both. It catalogs the mnemonic command letters (`q`uaff, `w`ear/wield, `e`at, `d`rop, `i`nventory, `l`ook), the use of Shift/Ctrl/Alt modifiers to extend the command space, and NetHack's `#` extended-command prefix for rare actions ([RogueBasin: Preferred Key Controls](https://www.roguebasin.com/index.php/Preferred_Key_Controls), [mirror](https://chizaruu.github.io/roguebasin/preferred_key_controls)).

**FAQ Friday.** r/roguelikedev's recurring "FAQ Friday" series (co-run by Kyzrati) is the community's documented dev-craft archive, indexed on RogueBasin's [Articles page](https://www.roguebasin.com/index.php/Articles); UI-relevant installments cover messages, world representation, and UI design, and Ge's own blog posts (Message Log, Map Dynamics, ASCII vs. Tiles above) originated as expanded FAQ Friday answers ([RogueBasin: Kyzrati](https://roguebasin.com/index.php/Kyzrati)).

**Tutorials/onboarding as UI.** Ge's [Tutorials and Help](https://www.gridsagegames.com/blog/2016/07/tutorials-help/) documents easing players in via context-sensitive help rather than front-loaded documentation, part of the same accessibility program as mouse support and tiles.
