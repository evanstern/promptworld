# Observe-Intervene Onboarding

How games whose players mostly *watch* explain the watching/acting split to newcomers
([[_grounding]] § Area 6). The evidence is strongest for idle/incremental games, weakest
for god games proper.

## Idle games: the UI itself is the tutorial

- First minutes offer exactly one action on a near-empty screen: Candy Box 2 opens with a
  candy counter and one button ("Eat all the candies"); Cookie Clicker opens with "a huge
  cookie… just begging to be clicked on" ([[_grounding]] § idle onboarding).
- Everything else arrives by **progressive disclosure**: new buttons appear as progress
  allows; store items are blacked out until affordable, then "the first item in the list
  glows." Players learn the interaction grammar itself — "glowing icons have new
  interactions, while greyed-out ones are temporarily unavailable" — instead of reading
  instructions ([Justin Chong: Onboarding in Incremental Games](https://zencron.medium.com/onboarding-in-incremental-games-bdfd9ea23e7b)).
- Genre advice warns that dumping systems early "causes new players to bail — instead, use
  UI to unlock complexity progressively" ([[_grounding]]).
- The observe half is paced deliberately: systems have "exponentially longer optimal wait
  times," so players self-select a check-in rhythm — "some players might play all day
  checking in every 15 minutes, while others only check their phone before bedtime"
  ([Eric Guan: Idle Game Design Principles](https://ericguan.substack.com/p/idle-game-design-principles)).

## God games: thin, mostly community-supplied onboarding

WorldBox's replayable tutorial hides behind a "Tutorial Bear" inside the "Other various
powers" menu; a player who missed the intro reported "I would have never thought to look
there" ([[_grounding]] § WorldBox). The loop is marketed as "watch miniature civilizations
do their thing or aid them," but no first-party writing was found on *teaching* that split
— community beginner guides carry the load. This sub-area is recorded as thin
([[Brief-and-Assumptions]]).

## Pure observation as a designed stance

- Progress Quest (2002), the idle-genre precursor, "doesn't even require clicks — you just
  run it and it plays itself" — the zero-player framing ([[_grounding]] § pure observation).
- Mountain (2014) has "no direct control over the environment, and only ambient sounds and
  weather to observe," and is "designed to be played in the background while the player
  uses other applications" — observation positioned as the whole game rather than a mode
  to be explained ([[_grounding]]; [Game Developer on Mountain](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/there-is-nothing-to-do-in-oreilly-s-i-mountain-i---and-that-s-a-good-thing)).
