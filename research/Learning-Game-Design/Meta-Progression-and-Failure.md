# Meta-Progression and Failure

How run-based games persist progress across failures, and the design debate around whether
that persistence dilutes skill ([[_grounding]] § Area 4).

## The mechanism

Meta-progression is "a layer of persistent progression… above the run-level systems in a
roguelite: upgrades, unlocks, and permanent bonuses that carry over between attempts."
Its stated function is failure-reframing: in roguelites "death delivers two signals: you
made an error, and you still made progress" ([[_grounding]] § definitions).

## Meta-progression as a paced tutorial

- Slay the Spire's unlock ladder is explicitly tutorial-shaped: run XP unlocks "three or
  four cards/relics each 'level'… added to the pool of potential pickups for future runs,
  not added to your starting deck." Design commentary frames this as the "Inverted pyramid
  of decision making" — "Start with simple mechanics… to help player learn and then
  gradually introduce new mechanics" — and notes unlocks are "automatic and not dependent on
  meta currency or user decisions," keeping the learning pathway designer-controlled. The
  Ascension ladder (20 difficulty tiers unlocked by winning) "keeps the game loop the same
  but lengthens the enjoyment of the game drastically over time" ([[_grounding]] § StS).
- Practitioner advice states the gating rationale directly: putting complicated content
  "behind a meta progression wall… helps prevent overwhelming new players" ([[_grounding]]).

## Hades: failure as forward motion

Supergiant's stated goal was "how to take the sting of failure and reduce that as much as
possible, knowing that it inherently feels bad to die in a game," built on "the idea of a
game with a story that would only move forward" — death advances the narrative. God Mode
(damage resistance starting at 20%, +2% per death) preserves the loop while easing it;
Kasavin: "If you could just blow through it, what's interesting about the game goes away
because dying in this game and looping through it over and over is a really important part
of the experience," and difficulty settings "may need to be proprietary to the game"
([[_grounding]] § Hades; [Inverse interview](https://www.inverse.com/gaming/hades-god-mode-interview)).

## The skill-dilution debate

- **Traditional-roguelike position** (Josh Ge): traditional roguelikes "have little to no
  metaprogression — players grow through knowledge and skill rather than explicit benefits
  carried over from one run to the next"; the real carry-over is "metaprogression of the
  mind" ("We grow as players, expanding our understanding of the balance and mechanics"),
  and "permadeath and being unable to take back decisions serves to highlight true mastery"
  ([[_grounding]] § skill-dilution; [Grid Sage Games](https://www.gridsagegames.com/blog/2020/02/traditional-roguelike/)).
- **Critics** (community threads): stat-based meta-progression makes games "seem designed
  for you to lose until a certain point," repeating "the same game a hundred times while
  getting incrementally more powerful" ([[_grounding]]).
- **Defenders**: performance still pays inside each run ("you get more resources if you
  play well"), roguelites "democratized the genre," and opt-in difficulty (Hades' Heat)
  returns the challenge to skilled players ([[_grounding]]).
- Rogue Legacy is consistently named the archetype of purchased persistent upgrades; no
  first-party design source was found in this pass ([[_grounding]] § skill-dilution).
